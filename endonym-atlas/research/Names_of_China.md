[China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China "China") has many contemporary and historical designations given in various languages for the East Asian country known as 中国; 中國; _Zhōngguó_ ('Central State' or 'Middle Kingdom') in [Standard Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_Chinese "Standard Chinese"), a form based on the [Beijing dialect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beijing_dialect "Beijing dialect") of [Mandarin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandarin_Chinese "Mandarin Chinese").

The English name "China" was borrowed from Portuguese during the 16th century, and its direct cognates became common in the subsequent centuries in the West.[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWilkinson2015191-2) It is believed to be a borrowing from [Middle Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Persian "Middle Persian"), and some have traced it further back to the [Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit "Sanskrit") word चीन (_cīna_) for the nation. It is also thought that the ultimate source of the name China is the Chinese word _Qín_ (秦), the name of the [Qin dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qin_dynasty "Qin dynasty") that ultimately unified China after existing as a [state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qin_(state) "Qin (state)") within the [Zhou dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhou_dynasty "Zhou dynasty") for many centuries prior. However, there are alternative suggestions for the etymology of this word.

Chinese names for China, aside from _Zhongguo_, include _Zhōnghuá_ ([中华](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E4%B8%AD%E5%8D%8E "wikt:中华"); [中華](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E4%B8%AD%E8%8F%AF "wikt:中華"); 'Central [Hua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huaxia "Huaxia")'), _[Huáxià](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huaxia "Huaxia")_ (华夏; 華夏), _Shénzhōu_ ([神州](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E7%A5%9E%E5%B7%9E "wikt:神州"); 'divine state') and _Jiǔzhōu_ ([九州](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E4%B9%9D%E5%B7%9E "wikt:九州"); 'nine states'). While [official notions of Chinese nationality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhonghua_minzu "Zhonghua minzu") do not make any particular reference to ethnicity, common names for the [largest ethnic group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Han_Chinese "Han Chinese") in China are _Hàn_ ([汉](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E6%B1%89 "wikt:汉"); [漢](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E6%BC%A2 "wikt:漢")) and _Táng_ ([唐](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E5%94%90 "wikt:唐")). The [People's Republic of China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/People's_Republic_of_China "People's Republic of China") ( _Zhōnghuá Rénmín Gònghéguó_) and the [Republic of China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_China "Republic of China") ( _Zhōnghuá Mínguó_) are the official names of the two governments presently claiming sovereignty over "China". The term " [mainland China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mainland_China "Mainland China")" refers to areas under the PRC's jurisdiction, either including or excluding [Hong Kong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hong_Kong "Hong Kong") and [Macau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macau "Macau").

There are also names for China used around the world that are derived from the languages of ethnic groups other than [Han Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Han_Chinese "Han Chinese"): examples include " [Cathay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathay "Cathay")" from the [Khitan language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khitan_language "Khitan language"), and _Tabgach_ from [Tuoba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuoba "Tuoba"). The realm ruled by the [Emperor of China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emperor_of_China "Emperor of China") is also referred to as the [Chinese Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Empire "Chinese Empire").

### Zhongguo

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Names_of_China&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Zhongguo")\]

#### Pre-Qing

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Names_of_China&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Pre-Qing")\]

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/70/He_Zun_transcription.jpg/500px-He_Zun_transcription.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:He_Zun_transcription.jpg) [He _zun_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/He_zun "He zun") rubbing and transcription; framed is the phrase 宅𢆶𠁩或 (宅茲中國); _zhái zī zhōng yù_; 'inhabit this central state'.

_Zhongguo_ (中國; 中国; _Zhōngguó_) is the name for China most commonly used in [Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_language "Chinese language") in modern times. The name has ancient origin: the earliest appearance of this two-character term is on the [He _zun_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/He_zun "He zun"), a bronze vessel dating to 1038–c.1000 BCE, during the early [Western Zhou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Zhou "Western Zhou") period. The phrase "_zhong guo_" came into common usage in the [Warring States period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warring_States_period "Warring States period") (475–221 BCE), when it referred to the "Central States", the states of the [Yellow River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yellow_River "Yellow River") Valley of the Zhou era, as distinguished from the tribal periphery.[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-3) In later periods, however, _Zhongguo_ was not used in this sense. Dynastic names were used for the state in [Imperial China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_China "Imperial China"), and concepts of the state aside from the ruling dynasty were little understood.[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWilkinson2015191-2) Rather, the country was called by the name of the dynasty, such as " [Han](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Han_dynasty "Han dynasty")", " [Tang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tang_dynasty "Tang dynasty")", " [Great Ming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ming_dynasty "Ming dynasty")", " [Great Qing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qing_dynasty "Qing dynasty")", etc. Until the 19th century, when the globalizing world began to require a common legal language, there was no need for a fixed or unique name.[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-Zarrow-4)

As early as the [Spring and Autumn period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spring_and_Autumn_period "Spring and Autumn period"), __Zhongguo__ could be understood as either the domain of the capital or used to refer to the Chinese civilization _zhūxià_ (諸夏; 'the [various Xia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huaxia "Huaxia")')[\[5\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-5)[\[6\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-6) or _zhūhuá_ (諸華; 'various [Hua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huaxia "Huaxia")'),[\[7\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-7)[\[8\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-8) and the political and geographical domain that contained it, but [Tianxia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tianxia "Tianxia") was the more common word for this idea. This developed into the usage of the Warring States period, when, other than the cultural community, it could be the geopolitical area of Chinese civilization as well, equivalent to [Jiuzhou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jiuzhou "Jiuzhou"). In a more limited sense, it could also refer to the Central Plain or the states of [Zhao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhao_(state) "Zhao (state)"), [Wei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wei_(state) "Wei (state)"), and [Han](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Han_(Warring_States) "Han (Warring States)"), etc., geographically central among the Warring States.[\[9\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-9) Although __Zhongguo__ could be used before the [Song dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Song_dynasty "Song dynasty") period to mean the trans-dynastic Chinese culture or civilization to which Chinese people belonged, it was in the Song dynasty that writers used __Zhongguo__ as a term to describe the trans-dynastic entity with different dynastic names over time but having a set territory and defined by common ancestry, culture, and language.[\[10\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-10)

The term __Zhongguo__ was used differently in every period. It could refer to the capital of the emperor to distinguish it from the capitals of his vassals, as in [Western Zhou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Zhou "Western Zhou"). It could refer to the states of the [Central Plain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Plain_(China) "Central Plain (China)") to distinguish them from states in the outer regions. The _[Shi Jing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classic_of_Poetry "Classic of Poetry")_ defines __Zhongguo__ as the capital region, setting it in opposition to the capital city.[\[11\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-11)[\[12\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-12) During the [Han dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Han_dynasty "Han dynasty"), three usages of __Zhongguo__ were common. The _[Records of the Grand Historian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Records_of_the_Grand_Historian "Records of the Grand Historian")_ use _Zhongguo_ to denote the capital[\[13\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-13)[\[14\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-14) and also use the concepts __zhong__ ("center, central") and __zhongguo__ to indicate the center of civilization: "There are eight famous mountains in the world: three in [Man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanman "Nanman") and [Yi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dongyi "Dongyi") (the barbarian wilds), five in _Zhōngguó_" (天下名山八，而三在蠻夷，五在中國).[\[15\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-15)[\[16\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-16) In this sense, the term __Zhongguo__ is synonymous with _Huáxià_ (华夏; 華夏) and _Zhōnghuá_ (中华; 中華), names of China that were first authentically attested in the [Warring States period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warring_States_period "Warring States period")[\[17\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-17) and [Eastern Jin period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jin_dynasty_(266-420)#Eastern_Jin "Jin dynasty (266-420)"),[\[18\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-18)[\[19\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-19) respectively.

From the Qin to the Ming dynasty, literati discussed __Zhongguo__ as both a historical place or territory and as a culture. Writers of the Ming period in particular used the term as a political tool to express opposition to expansionist policies that incorporated foreigners into the empire.[\[20\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-20) In contrast, foreign conquerors typically avoided discussions of __Zhongguo__ and instead defined membership in their empires to include both Han and non-Han peoples.[\[21\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-21)

#### Qing

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Names_of_China&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Qing")\]

__Zhongguo__ appeared in a formal international legal document for the first time during the Qing dynasty in the [Treaty of Nerchinsk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Nerchinsk "Treaty of Nerchinsk"), 1689\. The term was then used in communications with other states and in treaties. The Manchu rulers [incorporated Inner Asian polities into their empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qing_dynasty_in_Inner_Asia "Qing dynasty in Inner Asia"), and [Wei Yuan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wei_Yuan "Wei Yuan"), a statecraft scholar, distinguished the new territories from __Zhongguo__, which he defined as the 17 provinces of " [China proper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China_proper "China proper")" plus the Manchu homelands in the Northeast. By the late 19th century, the term had emerged as a common name for the whole country. The empire was sometimes referred to as Great Qing but increasingly as __Zhongguo__.[\[22\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-22)

_Dulimbai Gurun_ is the [Manchu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchu_language "Manchu language") name for China, with "Dulimbai" meaning "central" or "middle" and "Gurun" meaning "nation" or "state".[\[23\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-23)[\[24\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-24)[\[25\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-25) The historian Zhao Gang writes that "not long after the collapse of the Ming, China became the equivalent of Great Qing (_Da Qing_)—another official title of the Qing state," and "Qing and China became interchangeable official titles, and the latter often appeared as a substitute for the former in official documents."[\[26\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-FOOTNOTEZhao20067-26) The Qing dynasty referred to their realm as " _Dulimbai Gurun_" in Manchu. The Qing equated the lands of the Qing realm (including present-day [Manchuria, Xinjiang, Mongolia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qing_dynasty_in_Inner_Asia "Qing dynasty in Inner Asia"), Tibet, and other areas) with "China" in both the Chinese and Manchu languages, defining China as a [multi-ethnic state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multi-ethnic_state "Multi-ethnic state"), rejecting the idea that China only meant Han areas; both Han and non-Han peoples were part of "China". Officials used "China" (though not exclusively) in official documents, international treaties, and foreign affairs, and the "Chinese language" _Dulimbai gurun i bithe_ referred to Chinese, Manchu, and Mongol languages, and the term "Chinese people" (中國人; _Zhōngguórén_; _Dulimbai gurun i niyalma_) referred to all Han, Manchus, and Mongol subjects of the Qing.[\[27\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-FOOTNOTEZhao2006[httpswebarchiveorgweb20140325231543httpswebspaceutexaseduhl4958perspectivesZhao20-20reinventing20chinapdf_4,_7%E2%80%9310,_12%E2%80%9314]-27) Ming loyalist Han literati held to defining the old Ming borders as China and using "foreigner" to describe [minorities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnic_minorities_in_China "Ethnic minorities in China") under Qing rule such as the Mongols and Tibetans, as part of their anti-Qing ideology.[\[28\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-28)

When the Qing [conquered Dzungaria in 1759](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ten_Great_Campaigns#Three_campaigns_against_the_Dzungars_and_the_pacification_of_Xinjiang_(1755%E2%80%931759) "Ten Great Campaigns"), they proclaimed that the new land was absorbed into _Dulimbai Gurun_ in a Manchu language memorial.[\[29\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-29)[\[30\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-30)[\[31\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-31) The Qing expounded on their ideology that they were bringing together the "outer" non-Han Chinese, like the Tibetans, Inner, Eastern, and Oirat Mongols, together with the "inner" Han Chinese, into "one family", united in the Qing state, showing that the diverse subjects of the Qing were all part of one family. The Qing used the phrase " _Zhōngwài yījiā_" ([中外](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E4%B8%AD%E5%A4%96 "wikt:中外") [一家](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E4%B8%80%E5%AE%B6 "wikt:一家"); 'China and other \[countries\] as one family') or " _Nèiwài yījiā_" ([內外](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E5%85%A7%E5%A4%96 "wikt:內外") 一家; 'Interior and exterior as one family'), to convey this idea of "unification" of the different peoples.[\[32\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-32) A Manchu-language version of a treaty with the Russian Empire concerning criminal jurisdiction over bandits called people from the Qing "people of the Central Kingdom (_Dulimbai Gurun_)".[\[33\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-33)[\[34\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-34)[\[35\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-35)[\[36\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-36) In the Manchu official [Tulisen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tulisen "Tulisen")'s Manchu language [account of his meeting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narrative_of_the_Chinese_Embassy_to_the_Khan_of_the_Tourgouth_Tartars "Narrative of the Chinese Embassy to the Khan of the Tourgouth Tartars") with the [Torghut Mongol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalmyk_people "Kalmyk people") leader [Ayuki Khan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayuka_Khan "Ayuka Khan"), it was mentioned that while the Torghuts were unlike the Russians, the "people of the Central Kingdom" (_dulimba-i gurun_ _/_ 中國; _Zhōngguó_) were like the Torghut Mongols, and the "people of the Central Kingdom" referred to the Manchus.[\[37\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-37)

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/36/Fourmont-Zhongguo-Guanhua.png/250px-Fourmont-Zhongguo-Guanhua.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Fourmont-Zhongguo-Guanhua.png)"Middle Kingdom's Common Speech" ( _Medii Regni Communis Loquela_, 中國官話; _Zhongguo Guanhua_), the frontispiece of an early Chinese grammar published by [Étienne Fourmont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89tienne_Fourmont "Étienne Fourmont") in 1742[\[38\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-38)

The geography textbooks published in the late Qing period gave detailed descriptions of China's regional position and territorial space. They generally emphasized that China was a large country in Asia but not the center of the world. For example, the "Elementary Chinese Geography Textbook" (蒙學中國地理教科書) published in 1905 described the boundaries of China's territory and neighboring countries as follows: "The western border of China is located in the center of Asia, bordering the (overseas) territories of [Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom_of_Great_Britain_and_Ireland "United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland") and [Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Empire "Russian Empire"). The terrain is humped, like a hat. So all mountains and rivers originate from here. To the east, it faces [Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empire_of_Japan "Empire of Japan") across the [East China Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_China_Sea "East China Sea"). To the south, it is adjacent to the [South China Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_China_Sea "South China Sea"), and borders [French Annam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Indochina "French Indochina") and [British Burma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_rule_in_Burma "British rule in Burma"). To the southwest, it is separated from [British India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Raj "British Raj") by mountains. From the west to the north and the northeast, the three sides of China are all Russian territories. Only the southern border of the northeast is connected to [Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_Empire "Korean Empire") across the [Yalu River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yalu_River "Yalu River")." It further stated that "There are about a dozen countries in Asia, but only China has a vast territory, a prosperous population, and dominates East Asia. It is a great and world-famous country."[\[39\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-39)

The Qing enacted the first [Chinese nationality law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_nationality_law "Chinese nationality law") in 1909, which defined a Chinese national (Chinese:中國國籍; pinyin: _Zhōngguó Guójí_) as any person born to a Chinese father. Children born to a Chinese mother inherited her nationality only if the father was [stateless](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statelessness "Statelessness") or had unknown nationality status.[\[40\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-Shao5-40) These regulations were enacted in response to a 1907 statute passed in [The Netherlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Netherlands "The Netherlands") that retroactively treated all Chinese born in the [Dutch East Indies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_East_Indies "Dutch East Indies") as Dutch citizens. _[Jus sanguinis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jus_sanguinis "Jus sanguinis")_ was chosen to define Chinese nationality so that the Qing could counter foreign claims on [overseas Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overseas_Chinese "Overseas Chinese") populations and maintain the perpetual allegiance of its subjects living abroad through paternal lineage.[\[40\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-Shao5-40) A Chinese word called _xuètǒng_ (血統), which means "bloodline" as a literal translation, is used to explain the descent relationship that would characterize someone as being of Chinese descent and therefore eligible under the Qing laws and beyond, for Chinese citizenship.[\[41\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-Claytonp108-41)

[Mark Elliott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Elliott_(historian) "Mark Elliott (historian)") noted that it was under the Qing that "China" transformed into a definition of referring to lands where the "state claimed sovereignty" rather than only the [Central Plains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Plains_(China) "Central Plains (China)") area and its people by the end of the 18th century.[\[42\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-42)

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/09/%E6%BB%BF%E8%92%99%E6%BC%A2%E5%90%88%E7%92%A7%E6%95%99%E7%A7%91%E6%9B%B8_%28%E7%AF%80%E9%8C%84%29.png/500px-%E6%BB%BF%E8%92%99%E6%BC%A2%E5%90%88%E7%92%A7%E6%95%99%E7%A7%91%E6%9B%B8_%28%E7%AF%80%E9%8C%84%29.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:%E6%BB%BF%E8%92%99%E6%BC%A2%E5%90%88%E7%92%A7%E6%95%99%E7%A7%91%E6%9B%B8_(%E7%AF%80%E9%8C%84).png) Chapter _China_ (中國) of "The Manchurian, Mongolian and Han Chinese Trilingual Textbook" (滿蒙漢三語合璧教科書) published during the Qing dynasty: _"Our country China is located in [East Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Asia "East Asia")... For 5000 years, culture flourished (in the land of China)... Since we are Chinese, how can we not love China."_

Elena Barabantseva also noted that the Manchu referred to all subjects of the Qing empire regardless of ethnicity as "Chinese" ([中國](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E4%B8%AD%E5%9C%8B "wikt:中國") [之](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E4%B9%8B "wikt:之") [人](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E4%BA%BA "wikt:人"); _Zhōngguó zhī rén_; 'people of China'), and used the term (中國; _Zhōngguó_) as a synonym for the entire Qing empire while using _Hànrén_ ([漢人](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E6%BC%A2%E4%BA%BA "wikt:漢人")) to refer only to the core area of the empire, with the entire empire viewed as multiethnic.[\[43\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-43)

[William T. Rowe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_T._Rowe "William T. Rowe") wrote that the name "China" (中華; 中國) was apparently understood to refer to the political realm of the [Han Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Han_Chinese "Han Chinese") during the [Ming dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ming_dynasty "Ming dynasty"), that this understanding persisted among the Han Chinese into the early Qing dynasty, and that the understanding was also shared by [Aisin Gioro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aisin_Gioro "Aisin Gioro") rulers before the [Ming–Qing transition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ming%E2%80%93Qing_transition "Ming–Qing transition"). The Qing, however, "came to refer to their more expansive empire not only as the Great Qing but also, nearly interchangeably, as China" within a few decades of this development. Instead of the earlier (Ming) idea of an ethnic Han Chinese state, this new Qing China was a "self-consciously multi-ethnic state". Han Chinese scholars had some time to adapt this, but by the 19th century, the notion of China as a [multinational state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multinational_state "Multinational state") with new, significantly extended borders had become the standard terminology for Han Chinese writers. Rowe noted that "these were the origins of the China we know today.". He added that while the early Qing rulers viewed themselves as multi-hatted emperors who ruled several nationalities "separately but simultaneously", by the mid-19th century, the Qing Empire had become part of a European-style community of sovereign states and entered into a series of treaties with the West, and such treaties and documents consistently referred to Qing rulers as the " [Emperor of China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emperor_of_China "Emperor of China")" and his administration as the "Government of China".[\[44\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-44)

[Joseph W. Esherick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_W._Esherick "Joseph W. Esherick") noted that while the Qing Emperors governed frontier non-Han areas in a different, separate system under the [Lifanyuan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lifan_Yuan "Lifan Yuan") and kept them separate from Han areas and administration, it was the Manchu Qing Emperors who expanded the definition of __Zhongguo__ and made it "flexible" by using that term to refer to the entire Empire and using that term to other countries in diplomatic correspondence, while some Han Chinese subjects criticized their usage of the term and the Han literati [Wei Yuan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wei_Yuan "Wei Yuan") used __Zhongguo__ only to refer to the seventeen provinces of China and three manchurian provinces of the east, excluding other frontier areas.[\[45\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-45) Due to the Qing usage of treaties clarifying the international borders of the Qing state, they were able to inculcate in the Chinese people a sense that China included areas such as Mongolia and Tibet by education reforms in geography, which made it clear where the borders of the Qing state were, even if the populace didn't understand how the Chinese identity included Tibetans and Mongolians or what the connotations of being Chinese were.[\[46\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-46) The English version of the 1842 [Treaty of Nanking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Nanking "Treaty of Nanking") refers to "His Majesty the Emperor of China" while the Chinese refers both to "The Great Qing Emperor" (_Da Qing Huangdi_) and to __Zhongguo__ as well. The 1858 [Treaty of Tientsin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Tientsin "Treaty of Tientsin") contains similar language.[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-Zarrow-4)

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/95/Certificate_%28of_Identity%29_of_the_Imperial_Government_of_China_-_NARA_-_294991.jpg/250px-Certificate_%28of_Identity%29_of_the_Imperial_Government_of_China_-_NARA_-_294991.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Certificate_(of_Identity)_of_the_Imperial_Government_of_China_-_NARA_-_294991.jpg) Certificate of the Imperial Government of China issued by the Consulate General of Qing China at San Francisco in 1891

In the late 19th century, the reformer [Liang Qichao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liang_Qichao "Liang Qichao") argued in a famous passage that "our greatest shame is that our country has no name. The names that people ordinarily think of, such as Xia, Han, or Tang, are all the titles of bygone dynasties." He argued that the other countries of the world "all boast of their own state names, such as England and France, the only exception being the Central States",[\[47\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-47) and that the concept of _tianxia_ had to be abandoned in favor of _guojia_, that is, "nation", for which he accepted the term __Zhongguo__.[\[48\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-48) On the other hand, American Protestant missionary [John Livingstone Nevius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Livingstone_Nevius "John Livingstone Nevius"), who had been in China for 40 years, wrote in his 1868 book that the most common name which the Chinese used in speaking of their country was __Zhongguo__, followed by __Zhonghuaguo__ (中華國) and other names such as _Tianchao_ (天朝) and the particular title of the reigning dynasty.[\[49\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-49)[\[50\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-zhongguoname-50) Also, the Chinese geography textbook published in 1907 stated that "Chinese citizens call their country __Zhongguo__ or __Zhonghua__", and noted that China ( __Zhongguo__) was one of the few independent monarchical countries in the whole Asia at that time, along with countries like Japan.[\[51\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-51) The Japanese term " _[Shina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shina_(word) "Shina (word)")_" was once proposed by some as a basically neutral Western-influenced equivalent for "China". But after the founding of the [Republic of China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_China_(1912-1949) "Republic of China (1912-1949)") in 1912, __Zhongguo__ was also adopted as the abbreviation of _Zhonghua minguo_,[\[52\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-52) and most Chinese considered _Shina_ foreign and demanded that even the Japanese replace it with __Zhonghua minguo__, or simply __Zhongguo__.[\[53\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-53)

Before the signing of the [Sino-Japanese Friendship and Trade Treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sino-Japanese_Friendship_and_Trade_Treaty "Sino-Japanese Friendship and Trade Treaty") in 1871, the first treaty between Qing China and the [Empire of Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empire_of_Japan "Empire of Japan"), Japanese representatives once raised objections to China's use of the term __Zhongguo__ in the treaty, partly in response to China's earlier objections for the term _Tennō_ or [Emperor of Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emperor_of_Japan "Emperor of Japan") to be used in the treaty, declaring that the term __Zhongguo__ was "meant to compare with the frontier areas of the country" and insisted that only "Great Qing" be used for the Qing in the Chinese version of the treaty. However, this was firmly rejected by the Qing representatives: "Our country China has been called __Zhongguo__ for a long time since ancient times. We have signed treaties with various countries, and while _Great Qing_ did appear in the first lines of such treaties, in the body of the treaties _Zhongguo_ was always being used. There has never been a precedent for changing the country name" (我中華之稱中國，自上古迄今，由來已久。即與各國立約，首書寫大清國字樣，其條款內皆稱中國，從無寫改國號之例). The Chinese representatives believed that __Zhongguo__ as a country name equivalent to "Great Qing" could naturally be used internationally, which could not be changed. In the end, both sides agreed that while in the first lines "Great Qing" would be used, whether the Chinese text in the body of the treaty would use the term __Zhongguo__ in the same manner as "Great Qing" would be up to China's discretion.[\[50\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-zhongguoname-50)[\[54\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-54)

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2e/Big_Dragon_stamps.jpg/250px-Big_Dragon_stamps.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Big_Dragon_stamps.jpg) Qing postal stamps released in 1878

Qing official Zhang Deyi once objected to the western European name "China" and said that China referred to itself as __Zhonghua__.[\[55\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-LIULiu2009-55) However, the Qing established [legations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legation "Legation") and [consulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consulate "Consulate") known as the "Chinese Legation", "Imperial Consulate of China", "Imperial Chinese Consulate (General)" or similar names in [various countries with diplomatic relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dates_of_establishment_of_diplomatic_relations_with_the_Qing_dynasty "Dates of establishment of diplomatic relations with the Qing dynasty"), such as the United Kingdom and United States. Both English and Chinese terms, such as "China" and " __Zhongguo__", were frequently used by Qing legations and consulates there to refer to the Qing state during their diplomatic correspondences with foreign states.[\[56\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-56) Chinese diplomat [Zeng Jize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeng_Jize "Zeng Jize")'s 1887 profile, "China: the Sleep, and the Awakening", originally published in the _Asiatic Quarterly Review_, was widely noticed in the West.[\[57\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-57) Since the late 19th century, the Qing joined various international conventions in the name of "China", including (but not limited to) the [First Geneva Convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Geneva_Convention "First Geneva Convention"), [Hague Conventions of 1899 and 1907](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hague_Conventions_of_1899_and_1907 "Hague Conventions of 1899 and 1907"), and the [Hague Convention on Hospital Ships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hague_Convention_on_Hospital_Ships "Hague Convention on Hospital Ships").[\[58\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-58)[\[59\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-59)[\[60\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-60) Moreover, the English name "China" was also used domestically by the Qing, such as in its officially released stamps since Qing set up a modern postal system in 1878. The [postage stamps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postage_stamp "Postage stamp") (known as 大龍郵票 in Chinese) had a design of a large dragon in the centre, surrounded by a boxed frame with a bilingual inscription of "CHINA" (corresponding to the Great Qing Empire in Chinese) and the local denomination "CANDARINS".[\[61\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-61)

During the late Qing dynasty, various textbooks with the name "Chinese history" (中國歷史) had emerged by the early 20th century. For example, the late Qing textbook "Chinese History of the Present Dynasty" published in 1910 stated that "the history of our present dynasty is part of the history of China, that is, the most recent history in its whole history. China was founded as a country [5,000 years ago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_thousand_years_of_Chinese_civilization "Five thousand years of Chinese civilization") and has the longest history in the world. And its culture is the best among all the Eastern countries since ancient times. Its territory covers about 90% of East Asia, and its rise and fall can affect the general trend of the countries in Asia...".[\[50\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-zhongguoname-50)[\[62\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-62) After the [May Fourth Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/May_Fourth_Movement "May Fourth Movement") in 1919, educated students began to spread the concept of __Zhonghua__, which represented the people, including [55 minority ethnic groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnic_minorities_in_China "Ethnic minorities in China") and the Han Chinese, with a single culture identifying themselves as "Chinese". The Republic of China and the People's Republic of China both used __Zhonghua__ in their official names. Thus, __Zhongguo__ became the common name for both governments and _Zhōngguó rén_ (中国人; 中國人) for their citizens. [Overseas Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overseas_Chinese "Overseas Chinese") are referred to as _huáqiáo_ (华侨; 華僑; 'Chinese overseas'), or _huáyì_ (华裔; 華裔; 'Chinese descendants'), i.e. Chinese children born overseas.

#### Middle Kingdom

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Names_of_China&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: Middle Kingdom")\]

The English translation of _Zhongyuan_ as the "Middle Kingdom" entered European languages through the Portuguese in the 16th century and became popular in the mid-19th century. By the 20th century, the term was thoroughly entrenched in the English language, reflecting the Western view of China as the inward-looking Middle Kingdom, or more accurately, the "Central Kingdom" or _"_ Central State _"_. Endymion Wilkinson points out that the Chinese were not unique in thinking of their country as central, although China was the only culture to use the concept for its name.[\[63\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-63) However, the term __Zhongguo__ was not initially used as a name for China. It did not have the same meaning throughout the course of history, ( [see above](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#Pre-Qing)).[\[64\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWilkinson2012191-64)

During the 19th century, China was alternatively, although less commonly, referred to in the west as the "Middle Flowery Kingdom",[\[65\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-65) "Central Flowery Kingdom",[\[66\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-66) or "Central Flowery State",[\[67\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-67) translated from _Zhōnghuáguó_ (中华国; 中華國),[\[68\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-68) or simply the " [Flowery Kingdom](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Flowery%20Kingdom "wikt:Flowery Kingdom")",[\[69\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-69) translated from _Huáguó_ (华国; 華國).[\[70\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-70)[\[71\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-71) However, some have since argued that such a translation (fairly commonly seen at that time) was perhaps caused by misunderstanding the _Huá_ (华; 華) that means "China" (or "magnificent, splendid") for the _Huā_ (花) that means "flower".[\[72\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-72)[\[73\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-73)

### Huaxia

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Names_of_China&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section: Huaxia")\]

Main article: [Huaxia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huaxia "Huaxia")

The name _Huáxià_ (华夏; 華夏) is generally used as a [sobriquet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sobriquet "Sobriquet") in Chinese text. Under traditional interpretations, it is the combination of two words that originally referred to the elegance of traditional Han attire and [the Confucian concept of rites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Li_(Confucianism) "Li (Confucianism)").

- _Hua_ _,_ which means "flowery beauty" (i.e., having beauty of dress and personal adornment 有服章之美，謂之華).
- _Xia_ _,_ which means greatness or grandeur (i.e., having greatness in social customs, courtesy, polite manners and rites/ceremony 有禮儀之大，故稱夏).[\[74\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-74)

In the original sense, _Huaxia_ refers to a confederation of tribes—living along the Yellow River—who were the ancestors of what later became the Han ethnic group in China.\[ _[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_\] During the Warring States (475–221 BCE), the self-awareness of the _Huaxia_ identity developed and took hold in ancient China.

### Zhonghua minzu

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Names_of_China&action=edit&section=7 "Edit section: Zhonghua minzu")\]

Main article: [Zhonghua minzu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhonghua_minzu "Zhonghua minzu")

_Zhonghua minzu_ is a term meaning "Chinese nation" in the sense of a multi-ethnic national identity. Though originally rejected by the PRC,\[ _[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_\] it has been used officially since the 1980s for nationalist politics.

### Tianchao and Tianxia

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Names_of_China&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section: Tianchao and Tianxia")\]

Main articles: [Celestial Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestial_Empire "Celestial Empire") and [Tianxia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tianxia "Tianxia")

_Tianchao_ ([天朝](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E5%A4%A9%E6%9C%9D "wikt:天朝"); [pinyin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinyin "Pinyin"): _Tiāncháo_), translated as 'heavenly dynasty' or 'Celestial Empire',[\[75\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-tianchao-75) and _Tianxia_ ([天下](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E5%A4%A9%E4%B8%8B "wikt:天下"); [pinyin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinyin "Pinyin"): _Tiānxià_) translated as ' [All under heaven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All_under_heaven "All under heaven")', have both been used to refer to China. These terms were usually used in the context of civil wars or periods of division, with the term __Tianchao__ evoking the idea that the realm's ruling dynasty was appointed by heaven,[\[75\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-tianchao-75) or that whoever ends up reunifying China is said to have ruled __Tianxia__, or everything under heaven. This fits with the traditional Chinese theory of rulership, in which the emperor was nominally the political leader of the entire world and not merely the leader of a nation-state within the world. Historically, the term was connected to the later [Zhou dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhou_dynasty "Zhou dynasty") (c.1046–256BCE), especially the [Spring and Autumn period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spring_and_Autumn_period "Spring and Autumn period") (eighth to fourth century BCE) and the [Warring States period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warring_States_period "Warring States period") (from there to 221 BCE, when China was reunified by Qin). The phrase __Tianchao__ continues to see use on Chinese internet discussion boards, in reference to China.[\[75\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-tianchao-75)

The phrase __Tianchao__ was first translated into English and French in the early 19th century, appearing in foreign publications and diplomatic correspondences,[\[76\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-mailt-76) with the translated phrase "Celestial Empire" occasionally used to refer to China. During this period, the term _celestial_ was used by some to refer to the subjects of the Qing in a non-prejudicial manner,[\[76\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-mailt-76) derived from the term "Celestial Empire". However, the term _celestial_ was also used in a pejorative manner during the 19th century, in reference to Chinese immigrants in Australasia and North America.[\[76\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-mailt-76) The translated phrase largely fell into disuse in the 20th century.

### Jiangshan and Shanhe

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Names_of_China&action=edit&section=9 "Edit section: Jiangshan and Shanhe")\]

The two names _Jiāngshān_ (江山) and _Shānhé_ (山河), both literally 'rivers and mountains', quite similar in usage to __Tianxia__, simply referring to the entire world, the most prominent features of which being rivers and mountains. The use of this term is also common as part of the idiom _Jiāngshān shèjì_ (江山社稷; 'rivers and mountains', 'soil and grain'), in a suggestion of the need to implement good governance.

### Jiuzhou

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Names_of_China&action=edit&section=10 "Edit section: Jiuzhou")\]

Main article: [Jiuzhou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jiuzhou "Jiuzhou")

The name _jiǔ zhōu_ ([九州](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E4%B9%9D%E5%B7%9E "wikt:九州")) means 'nine provinces'. Widely used in pre-modern Chinese text, the word originated during the middle of the Warring States period. During that time, the [Yellow River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yellow_River "Yellow River") region was divided into nine geographical regions. Some people also attribute this word to the mythical hero and king [Yu the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yu_the_Great "Yu the Great"), who, in the legend, divided China into nine provinces during his reign.

The name _Han_ (汉; 漢; _Hàn_) derives from the [Han dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Han_dynasty "Han dynasty") (206 BC–AD 220), which presided over China's first "golden age".. The Han dynasty collapsed in 220 and was followed by a long period of disorder, including the [Three Kingdoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_Kingdoms "Three Kingdoms"), [Sixteen Kingdoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sixteen_Kingdoms "Sixteen Kingdoms"), and [Southern and Northern dynasties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_and_Northern_dynasties "Southern and Northern dynasties"). During these periods, various non-Han ethnic groups established various dynasties in northern China. People began to use the term _Han_ to refer to the natives of North China, who, unlike the minorities, were the descendants of the subjects of the Han dynasty.

During the [Yuan dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuan_dynasty "Yuan dynasty"), subjects of the empire were divided into four classes: [Mongols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongols "Mongols"), [Semu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semu "Semu"), Han, and "Southerners". Northern Chinese were called _Han_, which was considered to be the highest class of Chinese. This class, _Han_, includes all ethnic groups in northern China, including [Khitan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khitan_people "Khitan people") and [Jurchen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jurchen_people "Jurchen people") who have, for the most part, sinicized during the last two hundreds years. The name _Han_ became popularly accepted during this time.

During the Qing, the Manchu rulers also used the name _Han_ to distinguish the natives of the [Central Plains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhongyuan "Zhongyuan") from the Manchus. After the fall of the Qing government, the Han became the name of a nationality within China. Today, the term "Han persons", often rendered in English as "Han Chinese", is used by the People's Republic of China to refer to the most populous of the 56 [officially recognized ethnic groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Chinese_nationalities "List of Chinese nationalities") in China.

The name _Tang_ ([唐](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E5%94%90 "wikt:唐"); _Táng_) comes from the [Tang dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tang_dynasty "Tang dynasty") (618–907) that presided over China's second golden age. It was during the Tang dynasty that South China was finally and fully [sinicized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinicized "Sinicized"). _Tang_ would become synonymous with China in Southern China, and it is usually Southern Chinese who refer to themselves as "People of Tang" ([唐人](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E5%94%90%E4%BA%BA "wikt:唐人"), [pinyin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinyin "Pinyin"): _Tángrén_).[\[77\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-77) For example, the sinicization and rapid development of [Guangdong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guangdong "Guangdong") during the Tang period would lead the Cantonese to refer to themselves as _Tong-yan_ ([唐人](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E5%94%90%E4%BA%BA "wikt:唐人")) in Cantonese, while China is called _Tong-saan_ ([唐山](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E5%94%90%E5%B1%B1 "wikt:唐山"); pinyin: _Tángshān_; lit.'Tang Mountain').[\[78\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-78) [Chinatowns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinatown "Chinatown") worldwide, often dominated by Southern Chinese, also became referred to as _Tang People's Street_ ([唐人街](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E5%94%90%E4%BA%BA%E8%A1%97 "wikt:唐人街"), Cantonese: Tong-yan-gaai; pinyin: _Tángrénjiē_). The Cantonese term _Tongsan_ (Tang mountain) is recorded in [Old Malay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Malay "Old Malay") as one of the local terms for China, along with the Sanskrit-derived _Cina_. It is still used in [Malaysia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaysia "Malaysia") and [Indonesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indonesia "Indonesia") today, at times in a derogatory sense.\[ _[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_\]

Among Taiwanese, _Tang mountain_ (Min-Nan: _Tng-soa_) has been used, for example, in the saying, "has Tangshan father, no Tangshan mother" (有唐山公，無唐山媽; [Pe̍h-ōe-jī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pe%CC%8Dh-%C5%8De-j%C4%AB "Pe̍h-ōe-jī"): _Ū Tn̂g-soaⁿ kong, bô Tn̂g-soaⁿ má_).[\[79\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-79)[\[80\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-80) This refers to how the Han people crossing the [Taiwan Strait](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiwan_Strait "Taiwan Strait") in the 17th and 18th centuries were mostly men, and that many of their offspring would be through intermarriage with [Taiwanese aborigine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiwanese_aborigine "Taiwanese aborigine") women.

In [Ryukyuan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Okinawan_language "Okinawan language"), [karate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karate "Karate") was originally called _tii_ (手, hand) or _karatii_ (唐手, Tang hand) because 唐ぬ國_too-nu-kuku_ or _kara-nu-kuku_ (唐ぬ國) was a common [Ryukyuan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ryukyu_Kingdom "Ryukyu Kingdom") name for China; it was changed to _karate_ (空手, open hand) to appeal to Japanese people after the [First Sino-Japanese War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Sino-Japanese_War "First Sino-Japanese War").

[Zhu Yu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhu_Yu_(author) "Zhu Yu (author)"), who wrote during the Northern Song dynasty, noted that the name "Han" was first used by the northwestern 'barbarians' to refer to China, while the name "Tang" was first used by the southeastern 'barbarians' to refer to China, and these terms subsequently influenced the local Chinese terminology.[\[81\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-81) During the Mongol invasions of Japan, the Japanese distinguished between the "Han" of northern China, who, like the Mongols and Koreans, were not to be taken prisoner, and the Newly Submitted Army of southern China, whom they called "Tang", who would be enslaved instead.[\[82\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-82)

### Dalu and Neidi

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Names_of_China&action=edit&section=13 "Edit section: Dalu and Neidi")\]

_Dàlù_ (大陸/大陆; [pinyin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinyin "Pinyin"): _dàlù_), literally "big continent" or "mainland" in this context, is used as a short form of _Zhōnggúo Dàlù_ (中國大陸/中国大陆, mainland China), excluding (depending on the context) Hong Kong, [Macau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macau "Macau"), or Taiwan. This term is used in official contexts on both the mainland and Taiwan when referring to the mainland as opposed to Taiwan. In certain contexts, it is equivalent to the term _Neidi_ (内地; [pinyin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinyin "Pinyin"): _nèidì_, literally "the inner land"). While __Neidi__ generally refers to the interior as opposed to a particular coastal or border location, or the coastal or border regions generally, it is used in Hong Kong specifically to mean mainland China, excluding Hong Kong, Macau, and Taiwan. Increasingly, it is also being used in an official context within mainland China\[ _[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_\], for example, in reference to the separate judicial and customs jurisdictions of mainland China on the one hand and Hong Kong, Macau and Taiwan on the other.

The term __Neidi__ is also often used in Xinjiang and Tibet to distinguish the eastern provinces of China from the minority-populated, autonomous regions of the west.

In 1949, China adopted its official name, **_Zhōnghuá Rénmín Gònghéguó_** (in the older postal romanization, **_Chunghwa Jenmin Konghokuo_**), or the "People's Republic of China" in English, which was adapted from the [Chinese Communist Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Communist_Party "Chinese Communist Party")'s short-lived [Chinese Soviet Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Soviet_Republic "Chinese Soviet Republic") in 1931. While originally, it was proposed at the [Preparatory Committee of the New Political Consultative Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preparatory_Committee_for_the_New_Political_Consultative_Conference "Preparatory Committee for the New Political Consultative Conference") to called the newly established state as the "People's Democratic Republic of China" (中华人民民主共和国), this was later shortened to just the "People's Republic of China". The possessive adjective harkens to the socialist doctrine of a [people's democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/People's_democracy_(Marxism%E2%80%93Leninism) "People's democracy (Marxism–Leninism)") and was also meant to provide a cleft with the preceding [republican regime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_China_(1912%E2%80%931949) "Republic of China (1912–1949)").[\[83\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-83)

The name **New China** has been frequently applied to China by the [Chinese Communist Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Communist_Party "Chinese Communist Party") as a positive political and social term contrasting pre-1949 China (the [establishment of the PRC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proclamation_of_the_People's_Republic_of_China "Proclamation of the People's Republic of China")) and the PRC. This term is also sometimes used by writers outside of mainland China.

The PRC was known to many in the West during the [Cold War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_War "Cold War") as " **Communist China**" or "Red China" to distinguish it from the [Republic of China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_China "Republic of China") which is commonly called "Taiwan", "Nationalist China", or "Free China". In some contexts, particularly in economics, trade, and sports, "China" is often used to refer to mainland China to the exclusion of Hong Kong, [Macau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macau "Macau") and [Taiwan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiwan "Taiwan").

[Sun Yat-sen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun_Yat-sen "Sun Yat-sen") coined the English name "Republic of China" in a 1904 pamphlet, _The True Solution of the Chinese Question_.[\[84\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-:02-84):29

In 1912, China adopted its official name, **_Chunghwa Minkuo_** (rendered in pinyin **_Zhōnghuá Mínguó_**) or in English as the "Republic of China", which has also sometimes been referred to as " [Republican China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Republic_of_China "History of the Republic of China")" or the "Republican Era" (民國時代), in contrast to the [Qing dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qing_dynasty "Qing dynasty") it replaced, or as " **[Nationalist China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nationalist_Government "Nationalist Government")**", after the ruling Chinese Nationalist Party ( [Kuomintang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuomintang "Kuomintang")). 中華 (_Chunghwa_) is a term that pertains to "China", while 民國 (_Minkuo_), literally "People's State" or "Peopledom", stands for "republic".[\[85\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-85)[\[86\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-86) The name stems from the party manifesto of [Tongmenghui](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tongmenghui "Tongmenghui") in 1905, which says the four goals of the Chinese revolution were "to expel the Manchu rulers, to revive __Chunghwa__, to establish a Republic, and to distribute land equally among the people."

Since the separation from mainland China in 1949 as a result of the [Chinese Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Civil_War "Chinese Civil War"), the territory of the Republic of China has largely been confined to the island of Taiwan and some other small islands. Thus, the country is often simply referred to as "Taiwan", although this may not be perceived as politically neutral. Amid the hostile rhetoric of the [Cold War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_War "Cold War"), the government and its supporters sometimes referred to themselves as "Free China" or "Liberal China", in contrast to the People's Republic of China, which was historically called the "Bandit-occupied Area" (匪區) by the ROC. In addition, the ROC, due to pressure from the PRC, uses the name " **[Chinese Taipei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Taipei "Chinese Taipei")**" (中華台北) whenever it participates in international forums or most sporting events such as the [Olympic Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_Games "Olympic Games").

Taiwanese politician Mei Feng had criticised the official English name of the state, "Republic of China", for failing to translate the Chinese character _Min_ ( [Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_Chinese_characters "Traditional Chinese characters"):民; English: people) according to Sun Yat-sen's original interpretations. According to him, the name should instead be translated as "the People's Republic of China", which confuses with the current official name of China under communist control.[\[87\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-87) To avoid confusion, the [DPP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party "Democratic Progressive Party") administration under [Chen Shui-ban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chen_Shui-ban "Chen Shui-ban") began to add "Taiwan" next to the nation's official name in 2005.[\[88\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-88)

## Names in non-Sinic records

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Names_of_China&action=edit&section=17 "Edit section: Names in non-Sinic records")\]

Names used in other parts of Asia, especially East and Southeast Asia, are usually derived directly from words in one of the [languages of China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Languages_of_China "Languages of China"). Those languages belonging to a former tributary or Chinese-influenced country have an especially similar pronunciation to that of Chinese. Those used in [Indo-European languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-European_languages "Indo-European languages"), however, have indirect names that came via other routes and may bear little resemblance to what is used in China.

### China

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Names_of_China&action=edit&section=18 "Edit section: China")\]

Further information: [Chinas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinas "Chinas")

English, most [Indo-European languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-European_languages "Indo-European languages"), and many others use various forms of the name _China_ and the [prefix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prefix_(linguistics) "Prefix (linguistics)") "Sino-" or "Sin-" from the Latin _Sina_.[\[89\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-89)[\[90\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-90) Europeans had knowledge of a country known in Greek as _Thina_ or _Sina_ from the early period;[\[91\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-yule-91) the _[Periplus of the Erythraean Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periplus_of_the_Erythraean_Sea "Periplus of the Erythraean Sea")_ from perhaps the first century AD recorded a country known as _Thin_ ( _θίν_).[\[92\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-middle_kingdom-92) The English name "China" itself is derived from [Middle Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Persian "Middle Persian") (_Chīn_ [چین](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%DA%86%DB%8C%D9%86 "wikt:چین")). The modern word was first used in Europe by Portuguese explorers of the 16th century – it was first recorded in 1516 in the journal of the Portuguese explorer [Duarte Barbosa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duarte_Barbosa "Duarte Barbosa").[\[93\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-93)[\[94\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-94) The journal was translated and published in England in 1555.[\[95\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-95)

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6a/CEM-09-Asiae-Nova-Descriptio-China-2510.jpg/250px-CEM-09-Asiae-Nova-Descriptio-China-2510.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:CEM-09-Asiae-Nova-Descriptio-China-2510.jpg) _China_ (referring to today's [Guangdong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guangdong "Guangdong")), _Mangi_ (inland of _Xanton_ ( [Shandong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shandong "Shandong"))), and _Cataio_ (located inland of _China_ and _Chequan_ ( [Zhejiang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhejiang "Zhejiang")), and including the capital _[Cambalu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khanbaliq "Khanbaliq")_, _[Xandu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shangdu "Shangdu")_, and a marble bridge) are all shown as separate regions on this 1570 map by [Abraham Ortelius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_Ortelius "Abraham Ortelius")

The traditional etymology, proposed in the 17th century by [Martin Martini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Martini "Martin Martini") and supported by later scholars such as [Paul Pelliot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Pelliot "Paul Pelliot") and [Berthold Laufer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berthold_Laufer "Berthold Laufer"), is that the word "China" and its related terms are ultimately derived from the polity known as [Qin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qin_(state) "Qin (state)") that unified China to form the Qin dynasty ( [Old Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Chinese "Old Chinese"): _\*dzin_) in the 3rd century BC, but existed as a state in west China since the 9th century BC.[\[91\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-yule-91)[\[96\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-Wade-96)[\[97\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-97) This is still the most commonly held theory, although the etymology is still a matter of debate according to the _[Oxford English Dictionary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_English_Dictionary "Oxford English Dictionary")_,[\[98\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-OED-98) and many other suggestions have been mooted.[\[99\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-99)[\[100\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-wade13-100)

The existence of the word _Cīna_ in ancient Indian texts was noted by the Sanskrit scholar [Hermann Jacobi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Jacobi "Hermann Jacobi") who pointed out its use in the Book 2 of _[Arthashastra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthashastra "Arthashastra")_ with reference to silk and woven cloth produced by the country of __Cīna__, although textual analysis suggests that Book 2 may not have been written long before 150 AD.[\[101\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-101) The word is also found in other Sanskrit texts such as the _[Mahābhārata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mah%C4%81bh%C4%81rata "Mahābhārata")_ and the _[Laws of Manu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manusm%E1%B9%9Bti "Manusmṛti")_.[\[102\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-wade-102) The Indologist [Patrick Olivelle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrick_Olivelle "Patrick Olivelle") argued that the word __Cīna__ may not have been known in India before the first century BC, nevertheless he agreed that it probably referred to Qin but thought that the word itself was derived from a Central Asian language.[\[103\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-Liu-103) Some Chinese and Indian scholars argued for the state of _Jing_ (荆, another name for [Chu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chu_(state) "Chu (state)")) as the likely origin of the name.[\[100\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-wade13-100) Another suggestion, made by Geoff Wade, is that the _Cīnāh_ in Sanskrit texts refers to an ancient kingdom centered in present-day [Guizhou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guizhou "Guizhou"), called [Yelang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yelang "Yelang"), in the south Tibeto-Burman highlands.[\[102\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-wade-102) The inhabitants referred to themselves as _Zina_ according to Wade.[\[104\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-104)

The term _China_ can also be used to refer to:

- a modern state, indicating the PRC or ROC;
- "Mainland China" (中国大陆; 中國大陸; _Zhōngguó Dàlù_), which is the territory of the PRC minus the two regions of Hong Kong and Macau;
- " [China proper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China_proper "China proper")", a term used to refer to the historical heartlands of China without peripheral areas like Manchuria, [Inner Mongolia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_Mongolia "Inner Mongolia"), Tibet, and [Xinjiang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xinjiang "Xinjiang")

In economic contexts, " [Greater China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_China "Greater China")" (大中华地区; 大中華地區; _Dà Zhōnghuá dìqū_) is intended to be a neutral and non-political way to refer to mainland China, Hong Kong, Macau and Taiwan.

Sinologists usually use "Chinese" in a more restricted sense, akin to the classical usage of __Zhongguo__, to the Han ethnic group, which makes up the bulk of the population in China and of the overseas Chinese.

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/94/CEM-11-Chinae-nova-descriptio-2521.jpg/250px-CEM-11-Chinae-nova-descriptio-2521.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:CEM-11-Chinae-nova-descriptio-2521.jpg) [Barbuda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luiz_Jorge_de_Barbuda "Luiz Jorge de Barbuda")'s 1584 map, also published by Ortelius, already applies the name _China_ to the entire country. However, for another century many European maps continued to show _Cathay_ as well, usually somewhere north of the [Great Wall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Wall "Great Wall")

### Seres, Ser, Serica

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Names_of_China&action=edit&section=19 "Edit section: Seres, Ser, Serica")\]

Main article: [Serica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serica "Serica")

_Sēres_ (Σῆρες) was the [Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_language "Ancient Greek language") and Roman name for the northwestern part of China and its inhabitants. It meant "of silk", or "land where silk comes from". The name is thought to derive from the Chinese word for silk, 丝; 絲; _sī_; [Middle Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Chinese "Middle Chinese") _sɨ_, [Old Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Chinese "Old Chinese")_\*slɯ_, per [Zhengzhang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhengzhang_Shangfang "Zhengzhang Shangfang")). It is itself at the origin of the Latin for "silk", _sērica_.

This may be a [back formation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Back_formation "Back formation") from _sērikos_ (σηρικός), "made of silk", from _sēr_ (σήρ), "silkworm", in which case _Sēres_ is "the land where silk comes from".

### Sinae, Sin

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Names_of_China&action=edit&section=20 "Edit section: Sinae, Sin")\]

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/23/PtolemyWorldMap.jpg/250px-PtolemyWorldMap.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:PtolemyWorldMap.jpg) A mid-15th century map based on Ptolemy's manuscript [Geography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geography_(Ptolemy) "Geography (Ptolemy)"). Serica and Sina are marked as separate countries (top right and right respectively).

_Sīnae_ was an ancient Greek and Roman name for some people who dwelt south of Serica in the eastern extremity of the habitable world. References to the Sinae include mention of a city that the Romans called _Sēra Mētropolis_, which may be modern [Chang'an](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chang'an "Chang'an"). The Latin prefix _Sino-_ as well as words such as _Sinica_, which are traditionally used to refer to China, came from _Sīnae_.[\[105\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-105) It is generally thought that _Chīna_, _Sīna_ and _Thīna_ are variants that ultimately derived from "Qin", the western Zhou-era state that eventually founded the Qin dynasty.[\[92\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-middle_kingdom-92) There are other opinions on its etymology. [Henry Yule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Yule "Henry Yule") thought that this term may have come to Europe through the Arabs, who made the China of the farther east into _Sin_, and perhaps sometimes into _Thin_.[\[106\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-yule2-106) Hence the Θῖνα (_Thîna_) of the author of the [Periplus of the Erythraean Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periplus_of_the_Erythraean_Sea "Periplus of the Erythraean Sea"), who appears to be the first extant writer to employ the name in this form; hence also the Σῖναι (_Sînai_) and Θῖναι (_Thînai_) of [Ptolemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemy "Ptolemy").[\[91\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-yule-91)[\[92\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-middle_kingdom-92)

Some denied that Ptolemy's Σῖναι (_Sînai_) really represented the Chinese as Ptolemy called the country Σηρική (_Sērikḗ_) and the capital Σήρα (_Sḗra_), but regarded them as distinct from Σῖναι (_Sînai_).[\[92\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-middle_kingdom-92)[\[107\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-107) [Marcian of Heraclea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcian_of_Heraclea "Marcian of Heraclea") reported that the "nations of the Sinae lie at the extremity of the habitable world, and adjoin the eastern [Terra incognita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terra_incognita "Terra incognita")". The 6th century [Cosmas Indicopleustes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmas_Indicopleustes "Cosmas Indicopleustes") refers to a "country of silk" called Τζίνιστα (_Tzínista_), which is understood as referring to China, beyond which "there is neither navigation nor any land to inhabit".[\[108\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-108) It seems probable that the same region is meant by both. According to [Henry Yule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Yule "Henry Yule"), Ptolemy's misrendering of the [Indian Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Sea "Indian Sea") as a closed basin meant that Ptolemy must also have misplaced the Chinese coast, leading to the misconception of Serica and Sina as separate countries.[\[106\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-yule2-106)

In the [Hebrew Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_Bible "Hebrew Bible"), there is a mention of the faraway country " [Sinim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinim "Sinim")" in the [Book of Isaiah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Isaiah "Book of Isaiah") 49:12 which some had assumed to be a reference to China.[\[92\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-middle_kingdom-92)[\[109\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-109) In Genesis 10:17, a tribes called the " [Sinites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinite "Sinite")" were said to be the descendants of [Canaan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canaan "Canaan"), the son of [Ham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ham,_son_of_Noah "Ham, son of Noah"), but they are usually considered to be a different people, probably from the northern part of [Lebanon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebanon "Lebanon").[\[110\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-110)[\[111\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-111)

### Cathay, Kitay

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Names_of_China&action=edit&section=21 "Edit section: Cathay, Kitay")\]

Main article: [Cathay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathay "Cathay")

These names derive from the [Khitan people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khitan_people "Khitan people") that originated in [Manchuria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchuria "Manchuria") and conquered parts of northern China during the early 10th century to form the [Liao dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liao_dynasty "Liao dynasty"), and dominated Central Asia during the 12th century as the [Kara Khitan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kara_Khitan "Kara Khitan") Khanate. Due to the long period of political relevance, the name _Khitan_ become associated with China. Muslim historians referred to the Kara Khitan state as _Khitay_ or _Khitai_; they may have adopted this form of _Khitan_ via the [Uyghurs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uyghurs "Uyghurs") of [Qocho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Qocho "Kingdom of Qocho"), in whose language the final
_-n_ or _-ń_ became _-y_.[\[112\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-112) The name was then introduced to medieval and early modern Europe through Islamic and Russian sources.[\[113\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-millward-113) In English and in several other European languages, the name "Cathay" was used in the translations of the adventures of [Marco Polo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marco_Polo "Marco Polo"), which used this word for northern China. Words related to Khitay are still used in many [Turkic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkic_languages "Turkic languages") and [Slavic languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavic_languages "Slavic languages") to refer to China. However, its use by Turkic speakers within China, such as the Uyghurs, is considered pejorative by the Chinese authority who tried to ban it.[\[113\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-millward-113)

There is no evidence that either in the 13th or 14th century, Cathayans, _i.e._ Chinese, travelled officially to Europe, but it is possible that some did, in unofficial capacities, at least in the 13th century. During the campaigns of [Hulagu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hulagu "Hulagu") (the grandson of [Genghis Khan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genghis_Khan "Genghis Khan")) in [Persia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_Empire "Persian Empire") (1256–65), and the reigns of his successors, Chinese engineers were employed on the banks of the [Tigris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tigris "Tigris"), and Chinese astrologers and physicians could be consulted. Many diplomatic communications passed between the Hulaguid [Ilkhans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ilkhan "Ilkhan") and Christian princes. The former, as the great khan's liegemen, still received from him their [seals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seal_(emblem) "Seal (emblem)") of state; and two of their letters which survive in the archives of France exhibit the vermilion impressions of those seals in [Chinese characters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_character "Chinese character")—perhaps affording the earliest specimen of those characters to reach western Europe.

### Tabgach

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Names_of_China&action=edit&section=22 "Edit section: Tabgach")\]

The word _[Tabgach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tabgach "Tabgach")_ came from the metatheses of _[Tuoba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuoba "Tuoba")_ ( _\*t'akbat_), a dominant tribe of the [Xianbei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xianbei "Xianbei") and the surname of the [Northern Wei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Wei "Northern Wei") emperors in the 5th century before sinicisation. It referred to Northern China, which was dominated by part-Xianbei, part-Han people.

This name is re-translated back into Chinese as _Taohuashi_ ( [Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_language "Chinese language"):桃花石; [pinyin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinyin "Pinyin"): _táohuā shí_).[\[114\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-114) This name has been used in China in recent years to promote ethnic unity.[\[115\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-115)[\[116\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-116)

### Taugast

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Names_of_China&action=edit&section=23 "Edit section: Taugast")\]

In the works of [Byzantine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire "Byzantine Empire") Historian [Theophylact Simocatta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theophylact_Simocatta "Theophylact Simocatta"), written in the early 7th Century, [Tang China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tang_dynasty "Tang dynasty") was referred to as _Taugast_ ( [Byzantine Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_Greek "Medieval Greek"): Ταυγάστ).[\[117\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-:0-117) This name is likely related to Tabgach.[\[117\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-:0-117)

### Nikan

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Names_of_China&action=edit&section=24 "Edit section: Nikan")\]

_Nikan_ ( [Manchu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchu_language "Manchu language"): ᠨᡳᡴᠠᠨ) was a Manchu ethnonym of unknown origin that referred specifically to the Han Chinese; the stem of this word was also conjugated as a verb, _nikara(-mbi)_, which meant 'to speak the Chinese language'. Since __Nikan__ was essentially an [ethnonym](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnonym "Ethnonym") and referred to a group of people rather than to a political body, the correct translation of "China" into Manchu is __Nikan gurun__, 'country of the Han'.\[ _[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_\]

This exonym for the Han Chinese is also used in the [Daur language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daur_language "Daur language"), in which it appears as _Niaken_ (\[njakən\] or \[ɲakən\]).[\[118\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-118) As in the case of the Manchu language, the Daur word __Niaken__ is essentially an ethnonym, and the proper way to refer to the country of the Han Chinese (i.e., "China" in a cultural sense) is __Niaken gurun__, while __niakendaaci__ is a verb meaning "to talk in Chinese".

### Kara

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Names_of_China&action=edit&section=25 "Edit section: Kara")\]

Japanese: _Kara_ (から; variously written as 唐 or 漢). An identical name was used by the ancient and medieval Japanese to refer to the country that is now known as [Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korea "Korea"), and many Japanese historians and linguists believe that the word _Kara_ referring to China and/or Korea may have derived from a [metonymic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metonymy "Metonymy") extension of the appellation of the ancient city-states of [Gaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaya_confederacy "Gaya confederacy").

The Japanese word _[karate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karate "Karate")_ (空手, lit. "empty hand") is derived from the [Okinawan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Okinawan_language "Okinawan language") word _karatii_ (唐手, lit. "Chinese/Asian/foreign hand/trick/means/method/style") and refers to Okinawan martial arts; the character for __kara__ was changed to remove the connotation of the style originating in China.[\[119\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-119)

### Morokoshi

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Names_of_China&action=edit&section=26 "Edit section: Morokoshi")\]

Japanese: _Morokoshi_ (もろこし; variously written as 唐 or 唐土). This obsolete Japanese name for China is believed to have derived from a _kun'yomi_ reading of the Chinese compound 諸越_Zhūyuè_ or 百越 _[Baiyue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baiyue "Baiyue")_ as "all the Yue" or "the hundred (i.e., myriad, various, or numerous) Yue," which was an ancient Chinese name for the societies of the regions that are now southern China.

The Japanese common noun _tōmorokoshi_ (トウモロコシ, 玉蜀黍), which refers to [maize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maize "Maize"), appears to contain an element cognate with the proper noun formerly used in reference to China. Although __tōmorokoshi__ is traditionally written with Chinese characters that literally mean "jade [Shu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shu_(state) "Shu (state)") [millet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proso_millet "Proso millet")", the etymology of the Japanese word appears to go back to "Tang _morokoshi_", in which _morokoshi_ was the obsolete Japanese name for China as well as the Japanese word for [sorghum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sorghum "Sorghum"), which seems to have been introduced into Japan from China.

### Mangi

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Names_of_China&action=edit&section=27 "Edit section: Mangi")\]

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/db/1837_Malte-Brun_Map_of_the_Mongol_Empire_in_Asia_and_Europe_-_Geographicus_-_AsiaMongol-mb-1837.jpg/250px-1837_Malte-Brun_Map_of_the_Mongol_Empire_in_Asia_and_Europe_-_Geographicus_-_AsiaMongol-mb-1837.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:1837_Malte-Brun_Map_of_the_Mongol_Empire_in_Asia_and_Europe_-_Geographicus_-_AsiaMongol-mb-1837.jpg) 1837 map of Mongol Empire, showing _Mangi_ in southern China

From Chinese _Manzi_ (蠻子, southern barbarians). The division of north and south China under the [Jin dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jin_dynasty_(1115%E2%80%931234) "Jin dynasty (1115–1234)") and [Song dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Song_dynasty "Song dynasty") weakened the idea of a unified China, and it was common for non-Han peoples to refer to the politically disparate North and South by different names for some time. While Northern China was called [Cathay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#Cathay_or_Kitay), Southern China was referred to as Mangi. _Manzi_ often appears in documents of the Mongol-led [Yuan dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuan_dynasty "Yuan dynasty") as a disparaging term for Southern China. The Mongols also called Southern Chinese _Nangkiyas_ or _Nangkiyad_, and considered them ethnically distinct from North Chinese. The word __Manzi__ reached the Western world as _Mangi_ (as used by Marco Polo), which is a name commonly found on medieval maps. The Chinese themselves considered __Manzi__ to be derogatory and never used it as a self-appellation.[\[120\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-120)[\[121\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-121) Some early scholars believed _Mangi_ to be a corruption of the Persian _Machin_ (ماچين) and Arabic _Māṣīn_ (ماصين), which may be a mistake as these two forms are derived from the Sanskrit _Maha Chin_ meaning Great China.[\[122\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-122)

### Sungsong

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Names_of_China&action=edit&section=28 "Edit section: Sungsong")\]

In [Filipino language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filipino_language "Filipino language"), _Sungsong_ was a historical and archaic name for China.[\[123\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-123)[\[124\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-124) In [Tiruray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teduray_language "Teduray language"), the name meant specifically [Hong Kong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hong_Kong "Hong Kong").[\[125\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-125) The name comes from [Proto-Malayo-Polynesian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Malayo-Polynesian "Proto-Malayo-Polynesian") \*suŋsuŋ, which meant "to go against wind or current". Its application to China in Philippine languages presumably is connected with sailing problems in reaching mainland China from the Philippines.[\[126\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-126)

## Sign names

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Names_of_China&action=edit&section=29 "Edit section: Sign names")\]

The name for China in [Chinese Sign Language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Sign_Language "Chinese Sign Language") is performed by trailing the tip of one's fingertip horizontally across the upper end of the chest, from the non-dominant side to the dominant one, and then vertically downwards.[\[127\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-127) Many [sign languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sign_languages "Sign languages") have adopted the Chinese sign as a loanword; this includes [American Sign Language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Sign_Language "American Sign Language"),[\[128\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-128) in which this has happened across dialects, from Canada[\[129\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-129) to California,[\[130\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-130) replacing previous signs indicating East Asian people's typical [epicanthic fold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epicanthic_fold "Epicanthic fold"), now considered offensive.[\[131\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-131)

Multiple other languages have borrowed the sign as well, with some modifications. In [Estonian Sign Language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estonian_Sign_Language "Estonian Sign Language"), the index finger moves diagonally to the non-dominant side instead of vertically downwards,[\[132\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-132) and in [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Sign_Language "French Sign Language")[\[133\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-133) and [Israeli Sign Language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israeli_Sign_Language "Israeli Sign Language"),[\[134\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-134) the thumb is used instead. Some other languages use unrelated signs.[\[135\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-135) For example, in [Hong Kong Sign Language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hong_Kong_Sign_Language "Hong Kong Sign Language"), the extended dominant index and middle fingers, held together, tap twice the non-dominant ones in the same handshape, palm downwards, in front of the signer's chest;[\[136\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-136) in [Taiwanese Sign Language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiwanese_Sign_Language "Taiwanese Sign Language"), both hands are flat, with extended thumbs and other fingers held together and pointing sideways, palms towards the signer, move up and down together repeatedly in front of the signer's chest.[\[137\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_China#cite_note-137)
