As shown in the picture, “Korea is located on the east of Lieyang (列陽, lit. ' [Yang side](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yin_and_yang#Meanings "Yin and yang") of the [Lie River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taedong_River "Taedong River")'), south of Haibei Mountains; Lieyang belongs to the [state of Yan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yan_(state) "Yan (state)")."[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-1)

There are various **names of Korea** in use today that are all derived from those of ancient Koreanic kingdoms and dynasties. The choice of name often depends on the language, whether the user is referring to either or both modern Korean countries, and even the user's political views on the [Korean conflict](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_conflict "Korean conflict").

The name _[Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korea "Korea")_ is an [exonym](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endonym_and_exonym "Endonym and exonym"), derived from _[Goryeo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goryeo "Goryeo")_ or _Koryŏ._[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-Mil-2) Both [North Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Korea "North Korea") and [South Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Korea "South Korea") use the name in English. However, in the [Korean language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_language "Korean language"), the two Koreas use different terms to refer to the nominally unified nation: _Joseon_ or _Chosŏn_ (조선;朝鮮) in North Korea and _Hanguk_ (한국;韓國) in South Korea.[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-Mil-2)[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-3)

## History

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Names_of_Korea&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: History")\]

See also: [History of Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Korea "History of Korea")

The earliest records of Korean history are written in [Chinese characters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_characters "Chinese characters") called [hanja](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanja "Hanja"). Even after the invention of [hangul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hangul "Hangul"), Koreans generally recorded native Korean names with hanja, by [translation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Translation "Translation") of meaning, [transliteration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transliteration "Transliteration") of sound, or even combinations of the two. Furthermore, the pronunciations of the same character may be somewhat different in Korean and its various dialects, and may have changed over time.

For all these reasons, in addition to the sparse and sometimes contradictory written records, it is often difficult to conclusively determine the original meanings or pronunciations of ancient names.

### Ancient history

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Names_of_Korea&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Ancient history")\]

#### Joseon

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Names_of_Korea&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Joseon")\]

Until 108 BC, northern Korea and part of [Manchuria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchuria "Manchuria") were controlled by [Joseon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Chos%C5%8Fn "Old Chosŏn").[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-Mil-2)
This region was later renamed to _Gojoseon_, with the addition of the prefix _Go-_ ([古](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E5%8F%A4 "wikt:古")), meaning "old" or "ancient," used as a convention to distinguish it from the later [Joseon dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseon_dynasty "Joseon dynasty"). In contemporaneous [Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China "China") records, Joseon was written as 朝鮮, which is pronounced in modern Korean as _Joseon_ (조선). Historically, these characters have been read in the Korean language as 됴션 _Dyosyen_; 조선 _Joseon_ is a very recent spelling, reformed to reflect recent changes in the phonology of the Korean language. The name Joseon is also now still used by North Koreans and [Koreans living in China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koreans_in_China "Koreans in China") and [Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zainichi_Koreans "Zainichi Koreans") to refer to the peninsula, and as the official Korean form of the name of [_Democratic People's Republic of Korea_ (Joseon)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Korea "North Korea"). [\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-4)[\[5\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-SCRIBD-5) Cognates of _Joseon_ are also used in many Asian languages, such as Japanese, Vietnamese, and Chinese, to refer to the Korean Peninsula.[\[5\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-SCRIBD-5)[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-Mil-2)[\[6\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-6)

Possibly the [Chinese characters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_characters "Chinese characters") phonetically transcribed a native Korean name, perhaps pronounced something like "Jyusin". Some speculate that it corresponds to Chinese references to the ethnic group variously known as 肅愼 (숙신, [suksin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suksin "Suksin")), 稷愼 (직신, Jiksin) or 息愼 (식신, Siksin), although these names likely describe the ancestors of the [Jurchen people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jurchen_people "Jurchen people").[\[7\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-7)[\[8\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-8)

Other scholars believe 朝鮮 was a _translation_ (like Japanese [kun'yomi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kun'yomi "Kun'yomi")) of the native Korean _[Asadal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asadal "Asadal")_ (아사달), the capital of Gojoseon: _asa_ being a hypothetical [Altaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altaic_languages "Altaic languages") root word for "morning", and _dal_ meaning "mountain", a common ending for Goguryeo place names (with the use of the character 鮮 "fresh" to transcribe the final _-dal_ syllable possibly having been based on the pronunciation of the ancient ancestor of Middle Korean _dɔl-_ \> Modern Korean 달 _dal-_ "sweet").[\[9\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-9)

An early attempt to translate these characters into English gave rise to the expression "The Land of the Morning Calm" for Korea,[\[10\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-10) which parallels the expression "The Land of the Rising Sun" for Japan. While the wording is fanciful, the essence of the translation is valid.[\[11\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-11)

#### Han

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Names_of_Korea&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Han")\]

Around the time of Gojoseon's fall, various chiefdoms in southern Korea grouped into confederacies, individually known as [Mahan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahan_confederacy "Mahan confederacy") (마한; 馬韓), [Jinhan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jinhan_confederacy "Jinhan confederacy") (진한; 辰韓), and [Byeonhan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byeonhan_confederacy "Byeonhan confederacy") (변한; 弁韓), and collectively referred to as the [Samhan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samhan "Samhan") (삼한;三韓;lit.'Three Han'). While it cannot be stated conclusively, this _Han_ is believed to have originated from a native Korean root word. That word may have carried the meaning "leader" or "great", as seen in related words _[maripgan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maripgan "Maripgan")_ ("king", archaic), _hanabi_ ("grandfather", archaic), and _Hanbat_ ("Great Field", an archaic name for [Daejeon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daejeon "Daejeon")).[\[5\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-SCRIBD-5)[\[12\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-Gwangju-12)

_Han_ was variously transliterated in Chinese records as 韓; _hán_ (한), 幹; _gàn_ (간), 刊; _kān_ (간), and 干; _gān_ (간). The Korean name Han is etymologically disconnected from both the Chinese state 韓; hán, despite sharing the same Chinese character, and the Han (漢; hàn) dynasty along with the associated ethnicity.[\[5\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-SCRIBD-5)[\[12\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-Gwangju-12)

Beginning in the 7th century, the name " [Samhan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samhan "Samhan")" became synonymous with the [Three Kingdoms of Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_Kingdoms_of_Korea "Three Kingdoms of Korea").[\[13\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-kyunghyang-13) According to the _[Samguk sagi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samguk_sagi "Samguk sagi")_ and _[Samguk yusa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samguk_yusa "Samguk yusa")_, [Silla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silla "Silla") implemented a national policy, "Samhan Unification" (삼한일통;三韓一統), to integrate [Baekje](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baekje "Baekje") and [Goguryeo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goguryeo "Goguryeo") refugees. In 1982, a memorial stone dating back to 686 was discovered in [Cheongju](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheongju "Cheongju") with an inscription: "The Three Han were unified and the domain was expanded."[\[13\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-kyunghyang-13) During the [Later Silla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Later_Silla "Later Silla") period, the concepts of Samhan as the ancient confederacies and the Three Kingdoms of Korea were merged.[\[13\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-kyunghyang-13) In a letter to an imperial tutor of the Tang dynasty, [Ch'oe Ch'i-wŏn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ch'oe_Ch'i-w%C5%8Fn "Ch'oe Ch'i-wŏn") equated Byeonhan to Baekje, Jinhan to Silla, and Mahan to Goguryeo.[\[14\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-chosun-14) By the [Goryeo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goryeo "Goryeo") period, Samhan became a common name to refer to all of Korea.[\[13\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-kyunghyang-13) In his Ten Mandates to his descendants, [Wang Geon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wang_Geon "Wang Geon") declared that he had unified the Three Han (Samhan), referring to the Three Kingdoms of Korea.[\[13\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-kyunghyang-13)[\[14\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-chosun-14) Samhan continued to be a common name for Korea during the [Joseon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseon "Joseon") period and was widely referenced in the [Annals of the Joseon Dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annals_of_the_Joseon_Dynasty "Annals of the Joseon Dynasty").[\[13\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-kyunghyang-13)

In China, the Three Kingdoms of Korea were collectively called Samhan since the beginning of the 7th century.[\[15\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-National-15) The use of the name Samhan to indicate the Three Kingdoms of Korea was widespread in the [Tang dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tang_dynasty "Tang dynasty").[\[16\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-kwon-16) Goguryeo was alternately called [Mahan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahan_confederacy "Mahan confederacy") by the Tang dynasty, as evidenced by a Tang document that called Goguryeo generals "Mahan leaders" (마한추장;馬韓酋長) in 645.[\[15\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-National-15) In 651, [Emperor Gaozong of Tang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emperor_Gaozong_of_Tang "Emperor Gaozong of Tang") sent a message to the king of Baekje referring to the Three Kingdoms of Korea as Samhan.[\[13\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-kyunghyang-13) Epitaphs of the Tang dynasty, including those belonging to Baekje, Goguryeo, and Silla refugees and migrants, called the Three Kingdoms of Korea "Samhan", especially Goguryeo.[\[16\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-kwon-16) For example, the epitaph of Go Hyeon (고현;高玄), a Tang dynasty general of Goguryeo origin who died in 690, calls him a "Liaodong Samhan man" (요동 삼한인;遼東 三韓人).[\[15\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-National-15) The [History of Liao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Liao "History of Liao") equates Byeonhan to Silla, Jinhan to Buyeo, and Mahan to Goguryeo.[\[14\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-chosun-14)

The "Han" in the names of the [Korean Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_Empire "Korean Empire"), _Daehan Jeguk_, and the [Republic of Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Korea "Republic of Korea") (South Korea), _Daehanminguk_ or _Hanguk_, are named in reference to the Three Kingdoms of Korea, not the ancient confederacies in the southern Korean Peninsula.[\[5\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-SCRIBD-5)[\[13\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-kyunghyang-13)[\[14\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-chosun-14)[\[12\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-Gwangju-12)

#### Goryeo

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Names_of_Korea&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: Goryeo")\]

Around the beginning of the [Common Era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_Era "Common Era"), remnants of the fallen Gojoseon were re-united and expanded by the kingdom of [Goguryeo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goguryeo "Goguryeo"), one of the [Three Kingdoms of Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_Kingdoms_of_Korea "Three Kingdoms of Korea"). [\[17\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-17) It, too, was a native Korean word, probably pronounced something like "Guri", transcribed with various hanja characters: 高句麗, 高勾麗, or 高駒麗 (고구려, Goguryeo), 高麗 (고려, Goryeo), 高離 (고리, Gori), or 句麗 (구려, Guryeo). The source native name is thought to be either \* _Guru_ ("walled city, castle, fortress"; attested in Chinese historical documents, but not in native Korean sources) or \* _Gauri_ ("center, middle"; cf. Middle Korean _\*gaβɔndɔy_ and Standard Modern Korean _gaunde_ 가운데).[\[18\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-18)[\[19\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-19)

The theory that Goguryeo referenced the founder's surname has been largely discredited (the royal surname changed from _Hae_ to _Go_ long after the state's founding). \[ _[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_\]

### Revival of the names

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Names_of_Korea&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section: Revival of the names")\]

In the south, the Samhan resolved into the kingdoms of [Baekje](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baekje "Baekje") and [Silla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silla "Silla"), constituting, with Goguryeo, the [Three Kingdoms of Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_Kingdoms_of_Korea "Three Kingdoms of Korea"). In 668, Silla unified the three kingdoms, and reigned as [Later Silla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Later_Silla "Later Silla") until 935. The name [Samhan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samhan "Samhan") became synonymous with the Three Kingdoms of Korea beginning in the 7th century, and by the Goryeo period it became a common name to refer to all of Korea.[\[13\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-kyunghyang-13)

The succeeding dynasty called itself [Goryeo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goryeo "Goryeo") ( [Korean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_language "Korean language"): 고려; [Hanja](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanja "Hanja"): 高麗; [MR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McCune%E2%80%93Reischauer "McCune–Reischauer"): _Koryŏ_), and regarded itself as the successor to [Goguryeo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goguryeo "Goguryeo") ( [Korean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_language "Korean language"): 고구려; [Hanja](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanja "Hanja"): 高句麗; [MR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McCune%E2%80%93Reischauer "McCune–Reischauer"): _Koguryŏ_).[\[20\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-20)[\[21\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-21)[\[22\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-22)[\[23\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-23) The name Goryeo was the shortened form of Goguryeo and was first used during the reign of [Jangsu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jangsu_of_Goguryeo "Jangsu of Goguryeo") in the 5th century. Through the [Silk Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silk_Road "Silk Road") trade routes, [Persian and Arab merchants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_in_Korea#Early_history "Islam in Korea") brought knowledge about Silla and Goryeo to [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/India "India") and the [Middle East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_East "Middle East"). Goryeo was transliterated into [Italian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_language "Italian language") as "Cauli", the name [Marco Polo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marco_Polo "Marco Polo") used when mentioning the country in his _Travels,_ derived from the [Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_language "Chinese language") form _Gāolí_.[\[24\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-24)[\[25\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-25)

In 1392, a new dynasty established by a military coup revived the name [Joseon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseon "Joseon") (조선; 朝鮮; _Chosŏn_), after the ancient state Gojoseon. The alternative name for this nation could have been Hwaryeong, but in the end, Taejo of Joseon decided to go with Joseon. The hanja for Joseon have been translated into English as "morning calm" and sometimes rather as "morning freshness" or "morning radiance"[\[26\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-26) and Korea's English nickname became **"The Land of the Morning Calm";** however, this interpretation is not often used in the Korean language, and is more familiar to Koreans as a [back-translation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Translation#Back-translation "Translation") from English.[\[27\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-Kotoba-27) Only the interpretation as "morning freshness" is plainly viable, with "morning calm" and "morning radiance" being rather fanciful interpretations. The nickname "Land of the Morning Calm" was coined by [Percival Lowell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Percival_Lowell "Percival Lowell") in his book, "Chosön, the Land of the Morning Calm," published in 1885.[\[27\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-Kotoba-27)[\[28\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-28)

In 1897, the nation was renamed _Daehan Jeguk_ (대한제국;大韓帝國;lit.'Great Han Empire', known in English as [Korean Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_Empire "Korean Empire")). _Han_ had been selected in reference to _Samhan_, specifically the Three Kingdoms of Korea, not the ancient confederacies in the southern Korean Peninsula.[\[13\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-kyunghyang-13)[\[14\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-chosun-14) So, _Daehan Jeguk_ (대한제국;大韓帝國) means it is an empire that rules the area of Three Kingdoms of Korea. This name was used to emphasize independence of Korea, because an empire cannot be a subordinate country.

### 20th century

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Names_of_Korea&action=edit&section=7 "Edit section: 20th century")\]

When the Korean Empire came under [Japanese rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korea_under_Japanese_rule "Korea under Japanese rule") in 1910, the name reverted to _Joseon_ (officially, the Japanese pronunciation _Chōsen_). During this period, many different groups outside of Korea fought for independence, the most notable being the **Daehanminguk Imsi Jeongbu** (대한민국 임시정부;大韓民國臨時政府;lit.'Provisional Government of the Great Han People's State'), known in English as the [Provisional Government of the Republic of Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provisional_Government_of_the_Republic_of_Korea "Provisional Government of the Republic of Korea") (民國 = 民'people' + 國country/state' = 'republic' in East Asian capitalist societies).[\[a\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-29)

Korea became independent after [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II") (1945) and the country was then [divided](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Division_of_Korea "Division of Korea").

In 1948, the South adopted the provisional government's name of **Daehanminguk** (대한민국;大韓民國; see above), known in English as the Republic of Korea (ROK).[\[29\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-Tertitskiy2018-30) _Daehanminguk_ is sometimes used by South Koreans as a [metonym](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metonymy "Metonymy") to refer to the Korean nation as a whole, rather than just the South Korean state.[\[30\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-thefreelibrary.com-31)[\[31\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-unloved-32)

Meanwhile, the North became **Chosŏn Minjujuŭi Inmin Konghwaguk** (조선민주주의인민공화국;朝鮮民主主義人民共和國), translated in English as the Democratic People's Republic of Korea (DPRK). Each component of the name was carefully selected. _Chosŏn_ was the natural choice for the short form, "Korea", since it had been used throughout the colonial period to denote the Peninsula. For the long form of the name, _Konghwaguk_ was used for republic because of its leftist connotations over _Minguk_. North Koreans wanted to adopt something that had already been used in the [Eastern Bloc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Bloc "Eastern Bloc") to borrow legitimacy. A choice was presented between a " [People's Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/People's_Republic "People's Republic")" and a " [Democratic Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Republic "Democratic Republic")", because they had been used in the names of the short-lived [Ukrainian People's Republic of Soviets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainian_People's_Republic_of_Soviets "Ukrainian People's Republic of Soviets") and the [Finnish Democratic Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finnish_Democratic_Republic "Finnish Democratic Republic"), respectively. "People's Republic" was favored by [Pak Hon-yong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pak_Hon-yong "Pak Hon-yong") of the [Communist Party of Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communist_Party_of_Korea "Communist Party of Korea") and it had already been used by the temporary [People's Republic of Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/People's_Republic_of_Korea "People's Republic of Korea") (PRK) formed in Seoul after liberation. "Democratic Republic", on the other hand, was associated with [Mao Zedong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mao_Zedong "Mao Zedong")'s concept of [New Democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Democracy "New Democracy"), which influenced [Kim Tu-bong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kim_Tu-bong "Kim Tu-bong") of the [New People's Party of Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_People's_Party_of_Korea "New People's Party of Korea"). After his party merged with the [Workers' Party of North Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Workers'_Party_of_North_Korea "Workers' Party of North Korea"), the concept found its way to [Kim Il Sung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kim_Il_Sung "Kim Il Sung")'s parlance. Kim began to speak of a "Democratic People's Republic". This was echoed by what the true authorities of the country, the [Soviet Civil Administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Civil_Administration "Soviet Civil Administration"), prescribed, albeit in different order: "People's-Democratic Republic" ( [Russian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_language "Russian language"): Народно-Демократическая Республика). Thus the name of the country became the "Korea(n) Democratic People's Republic" in Korean and "Korean People's-Democratic Republic" in Russian so that both parties could claim that they were behind the coining.[\[29\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-Tertitskiy2018-30)

## Current usage

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Names_of_Korea&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section: Current usage")\]

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d0/Korean_Peninsula.jpg/250px-Korean_Peninsula.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Korean_Peninsula.jpg) A South Korean map of the Korean Peninsula, using the official Korean name for South Korea and the colloquial South Korean exonym for North Korea. [Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korea "Korea") is called _Chosŏn_ (조선;朝鮮) in North Korea, and _Hanguk_ (한국;韓國) in South Korea.

### East Asia

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Names_of_Korea&action=edit&section=9 "Edit section: East Asia")\]

#### Korea

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Names_of_Korea&action=edit&section=10 "Edit section: Korea")\]

Today, South Koreans use _Hanguk_ (한국, 韓國) to refer to just South Korea[\[32\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-33) or Korea as a whole, _Namhan_ (남한, 南韓; "South Han") for South Korea, and _Bukhan_ (북한, 北韓; "North Han") for North Korea. South Korea less formally refers to North Korea as _Ibuk_ (이북, 以北; "The North"). South Koreans often refer to Korea as "uri nara" (우리나라), meaning "our nation" or "our country". In addition, the official name for the Republic of Korea in the Korean language is "Daehanminguk" (대한민국, 大韓民國; which is usually translated as "The Republic of Korea").[\[33\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-34)

North Koreans use _[Chosŏn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseon "Joseon")_,[\[34\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-35) _Namjosŏn_ (남조선, 南朝鮮; "South Chosŏn"), and _Pukchosŏn_ (북조선, 北朝鮮; "North Chosŏn") when referring to Korea, South Korea, and North Korea, respectively. The term _Pukchosŏn_, however, is rarely used in the north, although it may be found in pre-war sources, such as the [Song of General Kim Il Sung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Song_of_General_Kim_Il_Sung "Song of General Kim Il Sung"). In the 1970s, [Kim Il Sung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kim_Il_Sung "Kim Il Sung") suggested that in the event of a North Korean takeover of South Korea, "Koryo" ( [Korean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_language "Korean language"): 고려) could become the Korean name of the country.[\[35\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-36)[\[36\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-37) Starting from January 2024, after North Korean leader [Kim Jong Un](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kim_Jong_Un "Kim Jong Un") [stopped pursuing reunification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_reunification#Deteriorating_relations_(2020%E2%80%93present) "Korean reunification"), North Korea started referring to South Korea as _Hanguk_ and _Daehanminguk_ instead of _Namjosŏn_.[\[37\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-38)

In the tourist regions of North Korea and during official meetings between South Korea and North Korea, _Namcheuk_ (남측, 南側) and _Bukcheuk_ (북측, 北側), or "southern side" and "northern side", are used instead of _Hanguk_ and _Bukhan_.[\[38\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-39)

The [Korean language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_language "Korean language") is called _Hangugeo_ (한국어, 韓國語, referring to the Korean language) or _Hangungmal_ (한국말, 韓國말, referring to spoken Korean only) in the South and _Chosŏnŏ_ (조선어, 朝鮮語) or _Chosŏnmal_ (조선말, 朝鮮말) in the North. The Korean script is called [hangeul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hangul "Hangul") (한글) in South Korea and _Chosŏn'gŭl_ (조선글) in North Korea.[\[39\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-40) The [Korean Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_Peninsula "Korean Peninsula") is called _Hanbando_ (한반도, 韓半島) in the South and _Chosŏn Bando_ (조선반도, 朝鮮半島) in the North.[\[40\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-41)

#### Chinese-speaking areas

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Names_of_Korea&action=edit&section=11 "Edit section: Chinese-speaking areas")\]

In [Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_language "Chinese language")-speaking areas such as [China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China "China"), [Hong Kong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hong_Kong "Hong Kong"), [Macau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macau "Macau"), [Taiwan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiwan "Taiwan") and [Singapore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singapore "Singapore"), different naming conventions on several terms have been practiced according to their political proximity to whichever Korean government although there is a growing trend for convergence.

In the Chinese language, the Korean Peninsula is usually called _Cháoxiǎn Bàndǎo_ ( [simplified Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simplified_Chinese_characters "Simplified Chinese characters"):朝鲜半岛; [traditional Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_Chinese_characters "Traditional Chinese characters"):朝鮮半島) and in rare cases called _Hán Bàndǎo_ ( [simplified Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simplified_Chinese_characters "Simplified Chinese characters"):韩半岛; [traditional Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_Chinese_characters "Traditional Chinese characters"):韓半島). Ethnic Koreans are also called _Cháoxiǎnzú_ (朝鲜族), instead of _Dàhán mínzú_ (大韓民族). However, the term _Hánguó ren_ (韩国人) may be used to specifically refer to South Koreans.[\[41\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-KHJ-42)

Before establishing diplomatic relations with South Korea, the [People's Republic of China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/People's_Republic_of_China "People's Republic of China") tended to use the historic Korean name _Cháoxiǎn_ (朝鲜 "Joseon" or "Chosŏn"), by referring to South Korea as _Nán Cháoxiǎn_ (南朝鲜 "South Joseon").[\[41\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-KHJ-42) Since diplomatic ties were restored, China has used the names that each of the two sides prefer, by referring to North Korea as _Cháoxiǎn_ and to South Korea as _Hánguó_ (韩国 "Hanguk"). The Korean language can be referred to as either _Cháoxiǎnyǔ_ (朝鲜语) or _Hánguóyǔ_ (韩国语). The [Korean War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_War "Korean War") is officially called the _Kàngměi Yuáncháo Zhànzhēng_ (抗美援朝战争 "War to Resist America and Aid Joseon \[ _i.e._ Korea\]") although the term _Cháoxiǎn Zhànzhēng_ (朝鲜战争 "Joseon War" _i.e._ "Korean War") is also used in unofficial contexts.[\[42\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-43)[\[43\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-44)

[Taiwan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiwan "Taiwan"), on the other hand, uses the South Korean names, referring to North Korean as _Běihán_ (北韓 "North Han") and South Korean as _Nánhán_ (南韓 "South Han"). The [Republic of China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_China "Republic of China") previously maintained diplomatic relations with South Korea, but has never had relations with North Korea.[\[44\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-45) As a result, in the past, _Hánguó_ (韓國) had been used to refer to the whole Korea, and Taiwanese textbooks treated Korea as a unified nation. The [Ministry of Foreign Affairs of the Republic of China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Foreign_Affairs_of_the_Republic_of_China "Ministry of Foreign Affairs of the Republic of China") under the [Democratic Progressive Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party "Democratic Progressive Party") Government considered North and South Koreas two separate countries.[\[45\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-46)[\[46\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-47) However, general usage in Taiwan is still to refer to North Korea as _Běihán_ (北韓 "North Han\[guk\]") and South Korea as _Nánhán_ (南韓 "South Han\[guk\]") while use of 朝鮮 – which in Taiwan is not pronounced _Cháoxiǎn_ but _Cháo **xiān**_ – is generally limited to ancient Korea. The Korean language is usually referred to as _Hányǔ_ (韓語).

Similarly, general usage in [Hong Kong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hong_Kong "Hong Kong") and [Macau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macau "Macau") has traditionally referred to North Korea as _Bak Hon_ (北韓 "North Han") and South Korea as _Nam Hon_ (南韓 "South Han").[\[47\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-48)[\[48\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-49) Under the influence of official usage, which is itself influenced by the official usage of the [People's Republic of China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/People's_Republic_of_China "People's Republic of China") government, the mainland practice of naming the two Koreas differently has become more common.

In the Chinese language used in [Singapore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singapore "Singapore") and [Malaysia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaysia "Malaysia"), North Korea is usually called _Cháoxiǎn_ (朝鲜 "Chosŏn") with _Běi Cháoxiǎn_ (北朝鲜 "North Chosŏn") and _Běihán_ (北韩 "North Han") less often used, while South Korea is usually called _Hánguó_ (韩国 "Hanguk") with _Nánhán_ (南韩 "South Han\[guk\]") and _Nán Cháoxiǎn_ (南朝鲜 "South Chosŏn") less often used.

In [Hokkien](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hokkien "Hokkien") speaking areas of Chinese communities in places like Taiwan and around [Southeast Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southeast_Asia "Southeast Asia"), Korea is called _Hân-kok_ (韓國 "Hanguk") where North Korea is referred to as _Pak-hân_ (北韓 "North Han") and South Korea as _Lâm-hân_ (南韓 "South Han").[\[49\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-50) The above usage pattern does not apply for Korea-derived words. For example, Korean [ginseng](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ginseng "Ginseng") is commonly called _Gāolì shēn_ (高麗參, "Koryo ginseng").

#### Japan

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Names_of_Korea&action=edit&section=12 "Edit section: Japan")\]

In [Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan "Japan"), North Korea is called _Kita-Chōsen_ (北朝鮮) and South Korea _Kankoku_ (韓国).

However, [Japan-based Koreans aligned with North Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zainichi_Koreans "Zainichi Koreans") claim the name _Kita-Chōsen_ is derogatory, as it only refers to the northern part of Korean Peninsula, whereas the government used to claim sovereignty over its whole territory.[\[50\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-51) Pro-North organisations, such as the [Chongryon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chongryon "Chongryon"), use the name _Kyōwakoku_ (共和国; "the Republic") instead, but the ambiguous name is not popular among others. In 1972, Chongryon campaigned to get the Japanese media to stop referring to North Korea as _Kita-Chōsen_. This effort was not successful, but as a compromise most media companies agreed to refer to the nation with its full official title at least once in every article, thus they used the lengthy _Kita-Chōsen (Chōsen Minshu-shugi Jinmin Kyōwakoku)_ (北朝鮮(朝鮮民主主義人民共和国); "North Korea (Democratic People's Republic of Korea)"). By January 2003, this policy started to be abandoned by most newspapers, starting with _[Tokyo Shimbun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Shimbun "Tokyo Shimbun")_, which announced that it would no longer write out the full name,[\[51\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-52) followed by _[Asahi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asahi_Shimbun "Asahi Shimbun")_, _[Mainichi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mainichi "Mainichi")_, and _[Nikkei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Nikkei "The Nikkei")_.[\[52\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-53)

For Korea as a whole, _Chōsen_ (朝鮮 "Joseon"; historically annotated as テウセン _Teusen_, which should have been pronounced most often as \*\[ʨjeu.ɕjeɴ\]) is commonly used. The term _Chōsen_, which has a longer usage history than _Kankoku_, continues to be used to refer to the Korean Peninsula, the Korean ethnic group, and the Korean language, which are use cases that would not cause confusion between South Korea and North Korea. When referring to both North Korean and South Koreans, the [transcription](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcription_(linguistics) "Transcription (linguistics)") of [phonetic English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gairaigo "Gairaigo") _Korean_ (コリアン, _Korian_) may be used because a reference to a _Chōsen_ national may be interpreted as referring specifically to a North Korean national.

The [Korean language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_language "Korean language") is most frequently referred to in Japan as _Kankokugo_ (韓国語) or _Chōsengo_ (朝鮮語). While academia mostly prefers _Chōsengo_, _Kankokugo_ became more and more common in non-academic fields, thanks to the economic and cultural presence of South Korea. The language is also referred to as various combined terms, such as _Kankoku-Chōsen-go_ (韓国朝鮮語), _Chōsen-Kankoku-go_ (朝鮮・韓国語), "Kankokugo (Chōsengo)" (韓国語(朝鮮語)), etc. Some people refer to the language as _Koriago_ (コリア語), using the European name for Korea. This term is not used in ordinary Japanese, but was selected as a compromise to placate both nations in a euphemistic process called _[kotobagari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kotobagari "Kotobagari")_. Likewise, when [NHK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NHK "NHK") broadcasts a language instruction program for Korean, the language is referred to as _hangurugo_ (ハングル語; "hangul language"); although it is technically incorrect since [hangul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hangul "Hangul") itself is a writing system, not a language.[\[b\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-54) Some argue that even _Hangurugo_ is not completely neutral, since North Korea calls the writing system _Chosŏn'gŭl_, not _hangul_. _Urimaru_ (ウリマル), a direct transcription of _uri mal_ (우리말, "our language") is sometimes used by Korean residents in Japan, as well as by [KBS World Radio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KBS_World_Radio "KBS World Radio"). This term, however, may not be suitable to ethnic Japanese whose "our language" is not necessarily Korean.

_Uri_ (우리 "we/us/our") is the first-person plural pronoun and it is commonly used as a prefix in Korean terms to describe things that are Korean, such as _uri nara_ (우리나라, "our country") which is yet another name Koreans give their country.[\[53\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-55)

In Japan, those who moved to Japan usually maintain their distinctive cultural heritages (such as the Baekje-towns or Goguryeo-villages). Ethnic [Korean residents of Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koreans_in_Japan "Koreans in Japan") have been collectively called _Zainichi Chōsenjin_ (在日朝鮮人 "Joseon People in Japan"), regardless of nationality. However, for the same reason as above, the euphemism _Zainichi Korian_ (在日コリアン; "Koreans in Japan") is increasingly used today. _Zainichi_ (在日; "In Japan") itself is also often used colloquially. People with [North Korean nationality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nationality_Law_of_the_Democratic_People's_Republic_of_Korea "Nationality Law of the Democratic People's Republic of Korea") are called _Zainichi Chōsenjin_, while those with [South Korean nationality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Korean_nationality_law "South Korean nationality law"), sometimes including recent newcomers, are called _Zainichi Kankokujin_ (在日韓国人 "Hanguk People in Japan").[\[54\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-56)[\[55\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-57)

#### Mongolia

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Names_of_Korea&action=edit&section=13 "Edit section: Mongolia")\]

Mongolian people have their own word for Korea: Солонгос ( _Solongos_). In [Mongolian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongolian_language "Mongolian language"), _solongo_ may mean either " [rainbow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rainbow "Rainbow")" or " [mountain weasel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mountain_weasel "Mountain weasel") ( _Mustela altaica_, _heeriyn solongo_ "field/steppe _solongo_") or [Siberian weasel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siberian_weasel "Siberian weasel") ( _Mustela sibirica_, _oyn solongo_ "forest _solongo_")."[\[56\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-Kang-58) Another theory states that the name is probably derived from the [Solon tribe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solon_people "Solon people") living in Manchuria, a tribe culturally and ethnically related to the Korean people.\[ _[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_\] North and South Korea are, accordingly, Хойд Солонгос ( _Hoid Solongos_) and Өмнөд Солонгос ( _Ömnöd Solongos_). The authors of an article published in 2023 have related Mongolian _Solongos_ for "Korea" to the Mongolic word \* _solagaï_ ( _cf._ Khalkh Mongolian солгой "left-handed, a lefty; out of tune, sounding wrong"), which may in turn be from Turkic _[\*sōl](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Reconstruction:Proto-Turkic/s%C5%8Dl)_ "left.";[\[56\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-Kang-58) because "left" also means "east(ern)" in Mongolic languages (whereas "right" also means "west(ern)"), the authors suggest that this word may have been used to refer to "a foreign enemy force in the east," similar to Chinese 東夷 _[Dōngyí](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dongyi "Dongyi")_.

The name of either [Silla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silla "Silla") or its capital Seora-beol was also widely used throughout Northeast Asia as the ethnonym for the people of Silla, appearing \[...\] as **Solgo** or **Solho** in the language of the medieval Jurchens and their later descendants, the Manchus respectively. The plural of _Solho_ ("Korea, Korean; a Korean") in the Manchu language is _Solhoso_ (" [Koreans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koreans "Koreans"), Korean people"), similar to _Solongos_ in Mongolian. Manchu also has _solohi_ or _silihi_ for certain kinds of weasel (specifically, _suwayan solohi_ "yellow _solohi_" for _Mustela sibirica_), but _nioron_ for "rainbow."

The Mongolian and Manchu names for Korea and Koreans also resemble [Old Japanese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Japanese "Old Japanese") _Siraki_ ~ _Siragi_ (" [Silla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silla "Silla")") and Old Korean \* _Syerapeur_ "Gyeongju; capital city of Silla" > Late Middle Korean _Syeveulh_ "capital city (of Joseon)" > Modern Korean [Seoul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seoul "Seoul") "capital city (of South Korea)."

#### Vietnamese-speaking areas

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Names_of_Korea&action=edit&section=14 "Edit section: Vietnamese-speaking areas")\]

In Vietnam, people call North Korea __Triều Tiên__ (朝鮮; "Chosŏn") and South Korea __Hàn Quốc__ (韓國; "Hanguk"). Prior to unification, [North Vietnam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Vietnam "North Vietnam") used __Bắc Triều Tiên__ (北朝鮮; Bukchosŏn) and __Nam Triều Tiên__ (南朝鮮; Namjoseon) while [South Vietnam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Vietnam "South Vietnam") used __Bắc Hàn__ (北韓; Bukhan) and __Nam Hàn__ (南韓; Namhan) for North and South Korea, respectively. After unification, the northern Vietnamese terminology persisted until the 1990s. When South Korea reestablished diplomatic relations with Vietnam in 1993, it requested that Vietnam use the name that it uses for itself, and __Hàn Quốc__ gradually replaced __Nam Triều Tiên__ in usage.[\[57\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-59)

In the Vietnamese language used in the United States, Canada, Australia, and other (pro-)Western countries, _Bắc Hàn_ and _Nam Hàn_ remain in use and are most commonly used, since most of Vietnamese diaspora there were from southern Vietnam area.

### Outside East Asia

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Names_of_Korea&action=edit&section=15 "Edit section: Outside East Asia")\]

#### English usage and spelling

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Names_of_Korea&action=edit&section=16 "Edit section: English usage and spelling")\]

Both South and North Korea use the name "Korea" when referring to their countries in English. North Korea is sometimes referred to as "Democratic People's Republic of Korea" (DPRK) and South Korea is sometimes referred to as the "Republic of Korea" (ROK). The official names of both entities are also used by organizations such as [United Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations "United Nations"), [International Olympic Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Olympic_Committee "International Olympic Committee") and media such as the [Associated Press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associated_Press "Associated Press"), [China Global Television Network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China_Global_Television_Network "China Global Television Network") (CGTN), and several others.[\[58\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-60)

As with other European languages, "Corée" in French, for example, English historically had a variety of names for Korea. These included "Cauli" (Marco Polo's rendering of _Goryeo_), Caule, Core, Cory, Caoli, and Corai as well as two spellings that survived into the 19th century, [Corea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#English_usage_and_spelling) and Korea. The modern spelling, "Korea", first appeared in the late 17th century in the travel writings of the [Dutch East India Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_East_India_Company "Dutch East India Company")'s [Hendrick Hamel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hendrick_Hamel "Hendrick Hamel"). The terms "Chosunese" or "Chosonese" were first used to refer to the people of [Joseon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseon "Joseon") in the late 19th century but were eventually phased out.[\[59\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-61)

Both major English-speaking governments in the 19th and 20th centuries (the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States "United States") and the [United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom_of_Great_Britain_and_Ireland "United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland") and its [empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Empire "British Empire")) used both "Korea" and "Corea" until the early part of the [period of Japanese occupation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korea_under_Japanese_rule "Korea under Japanese rule").[\[60\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-Corea-62)[\[61\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-63) English-language publications in the 19th century generally used the spelling Corea, which was also used at the founding of the UK's embassy in Seoul in 1890.[\[62\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-Demick-64) However, at the turn of the century, the then U.S. [minister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_(diplomacy) "Minister (diplomacy)") and [consul general](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consul_general "Consul general") to Korea, [Horace Newton Allen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horace_Newton_Allen "Horace Newton Allen"), used "Korea" in his works published on the country.[\[63\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-65) At the official Korean exhibit at the [World's Columbian Exhibition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World's_Columbian_Exhibition "World's Columbian Exhibition") in Chicago in 1893 a sign was posted by the Korean Commissioner saying of his country's name that "'Korea' and 'Corea' are both correct, but the former is preferred."[\[64\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-columbian-66) This may have had something to do with Allen's influence, as he was heavily involved in the planning and participation of the Korean exhibit at Chicago.[\[64\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-columbian-66)

A shift can also be seen in Korea itself, where postage stamps issued in 1884 used the name "Corean Post" in English, but those from 1885 and thereafter used "Korea" or "Korean Post".[\[65\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-67)

By the first two decades of the 20th century, "Korea" began to be seen more frequently than "Corea" – a change that coincided with Japan's consolidation of its grip over the peninsula. However, the spelling "Corea" was occasionally used even under full colonial rule and both it and "Korea" were largely eschewed in favor of the Japanese-derived "Chosen",[\[60\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-Corea-62) which itself was derived from "Joseon".

A theory that grew in popularity in South Korea in the early 2000s and especially during the 2002 joint [World Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2002_FIFA_World_Cup "2002 FIFA World Cup") (and endorsed by the North Korean state) was that Japan as occupier had intentionally standardized the spelling on "Korea", allegedly so that "Japan" would appear first alphabetically. However, evidence of a deliberate name change orchestrated by Japanese authorities is circumstantial, for example, a 1912 memoir by a Japanese colonial official that complained of the Koreans' tendency "to maintain they are an independent country by insisting on using a C to write their country's name."[\[62\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-Demick-64)

#### Other languages

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Names_of_Korea&action=edit&section=17 "Edit section: Other languages")\]

European languages use variations of the name "Korea" for both North and South Korea. In general, [Celtic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celtic_languages "Celtic languages") and [Romance languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romance_languages "Romance languages") spell it "Corea" (or variations) since "c" represents the /k/ sound in most Romance and Celtic orthographies. However, languages that have a general preference towards representing /k/ with "k" rather than "c", such as most [Germanic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germanic_languages "Germanic languages") or [Slavic languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavic_languages "Slavic languages"), generally use variants of "Korea" instead.[\[66\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-68)[\[67\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-69) In languages using other alphabets such as [Russian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_language "Russian language") ( [Cyrillic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyrillic "Cyrillic")), variations phonetically similar to "Korea" are also used for example the Russian name for Korea is Корея, romanization Koreya or Koreja. Outside of Europe, most languages also use variants of "Korea", often adopted to local orthographies. Some Languages, especially [Romance Languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romance_Languages "Romance Languages") like [Portuguese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_language "Portuguese language"), [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language "French language") and [Spanish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_language "Spanish language") use spellings that start with "c": Coreia, Corée and Corea respectively.[\[68\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-70) "Korea" in the [Jurchen Jin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jin_dynasty_(1115%E2%80%931234) "Jin dynasty (1115–1234)")'s national language ( [Jurchen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jurchen_language "Jurchen language")) is "Sogo".[\[69\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-71)[\[70\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-72) "Korea" in the [conlang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constructed_language "Constructed language") [Esperanto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esperanto "Esperanto") is "Koreio". "Korea" in [Hmong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hmong_language "Hmong language") is "Kauslim" ("s" and "m" represent [tones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tone_(linguistics) "Tone (linguistics)"), not consonants).[\[71\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-73)

### Koreans abroad

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Names_of_Korea&action=edit&section=18 "Edit section: Koreans abroad")\]

See also: [Korean diaspora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_diaspora "Korean diaspora")

Emigrants who moved to [Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russia "Russia") and [Central Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Asia "Central Asia") call themselves _[Goryeoin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goryeoin "Goryeoin")_ or _[Koryo-saram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koryo-saram "Koryo-saram")_ (고려인;高麗人;lit.'person or people of Goryeo'), or _Koreytsi_ (корейцы) in [Russian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_language "Russian language"). Many Goryeoin are living in the [CIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commonwealth_of_Independent_States "Commonwealth of Independent States"), including an estimated 87,800 in [Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russia "Russia"),[\[72\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-74) 174,000 in [Uzbekistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uzbekistan "Uzbekistan"),[\[73\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-75) 17,000 in [Kyrgyzstan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyrgyzstan "Kyrgyzstan"),[\[74\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-76) 12,000 in [Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukraine "Ukraine"),[\[75\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-77) 400 in [Belarus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belarus "Belarus").[\[76\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-78)[\[77\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-79) As of 2025, there are also 1.7 million ethnic Koreans living in China who hold Chinese citizenship and a further 560,000 Korean expatriates from both North and South living in China.[\[78\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-80)

South Korean expatriates living in the U.S. may refer to themselves as [_jaemi gyopo_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_American "Korean American") (재미교포;在美僑胞;lit.'residents of America'), or "gyopo" for short.[\[79\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-81)

## Sobriquets of Korea

\[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Names_of_Korea&action=edit&section=20 "Edit section: Sobriquets of Korea")\]

In traditional Korean culture, as well as in the cultural tradition of East Asia, the land of Korea has assumed a number of [sobriquets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sobriquet "Sobriquet") over the centuries, including:

- 계림 (鷄林) _[Gyerim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gyerim "Gyerim")_, "Rooster Forest", in reference to an early name for [Silla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silla "Silla").[\[80\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-82)
- 군자지국 (君子之國) _Gunjajiguk_, or "Land of Scholarly Gentlemen".[\[81\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-83)[\[82\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-84)
- 금수강산 (錦繡江山) _Geumsu Gangsan_, "Land of Embroidered (or Splendid) Rivers and Mountains".[\[83\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-85)[\[84\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-86)[\[85\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-87)
- 단국 (檀國) _Danguk_, "Country of [Dangun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dangun "Dangun")".[\[c\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-88)
- 대동 (大東) _Daedong_, "Great East".[\[86\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-89)
- 동국 (東國) _Dongguk_, "Eastern Country".[\[d\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-90)
- 동방 (東邦) _Dongbang_, literally "an Eastern Country" referring to Korea.
- 동방예의지국 (東方禮義之國, 東方禮儀之國) _Dongbang yeuijiguk_, "Eastern Country of Courtesy".[\[87\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-91)[\[88\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-92)
- 동야 (東野) _Dongya_, "Eastern Plains".
- 동이 (東夷) _Dong-i_, "Eastern Foreigners".[\[89\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-93)[\[90\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-94)
- 구이 (九夷) _Gu-i_, "Nine-i", refers to ancient tribes in the Korean peninsula.[\[91\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-95)
- 동토 (東土) _Dongto_, "Eastern Land".
- 백의민족 (白衣民族) _[Baeguiminjok](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_clothing_in_Korea "White clothing in Korea")_, "The white-clad [folk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volk "Volk")".[\[92\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-96)[\[93\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-97)
- 삼천리 (三千里) _Samcheolli_, "Three thousand [li](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Li_(unit) "Li (unit)")", a reference to the length traditionally attributed to the country from its northern to southern tips plus eastern to western tips.[\[94\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-98)[\[95\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-99)
- 아사달 (阿斯達) _[Asadal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asadal "Asadal")_, apparently an [Old Korean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Korean "Old Korean") term for Joseon.
- 청구 (靑丘) _[Cheonggu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheonggu "Cheonggu")_, or "Azure Hills". The color Azure is associated with the East.
- 팔도강산 (八道江山) _Paldo Gangsan_, "Rivers and Mountains of the Eight Provinces", referring to the traditional [eight provinces of Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_provinces_of_Korea "Eight provinces of Korea").
- 근화향 (槿花鄕) _Geunhwahyang_, "Country of Mugunghwa" refer to Silla Kingdom.[\[96\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-100)
- 근역 (槿域) _Geunyeok_, "Hibiscus Territory", or Land of Hibiscus[\[97\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-101)
- 삼한 (三韓) _Samhan_, or "Three Hans", refers to [Samhan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samhan "Samhan") confederacy that ruled Southern Korea.[\[98\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-102) Beginning in the 7th century, Samhan became synonymous with the [Three Kingdoms of Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_Kingdoms_of_Korea "Three Kingdoms of Korea").
- 해동 (海東) _Haedong_, "East of the Sea" (here being the West Sea separating from Korea).[\[99\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-103)
- 해동삼국 (海東三國) _Haedong Samguk_, "Three Kingdoms East of the Sea" refers to [Three Kingdoms of Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_Kingdoms_of_Korea "Three Kingdoms of Korea")
- 해동성국 (海東盛國) _Haedong Seongguk_, literally "Flourishing Eastern Sea Country", historically refers to [Balhae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balhae "Balhae") Kingdom of north–south period.[\[100\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-104)[\[101\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-105)
- 진국 (震國,振國) _Jinguk_, "Shock Country", old name of [Balhae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balhae "Balhae") Kingdom.[\[102\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-106)[\[103\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-107)
- 진역 (震域) _Jinyeok_, "Eastern Domain".
- 진단 (震檀,震壇) _Jindan_, "Eastern Country of [Dangun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dangun "Dangun")".
- 진국 (辰國) _Jinguk_, "Country of Early Morning", refer to the Jin state of [Gojoseon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Chos%C5%8Fn "Old Chosŏn") period.[\[104\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-108)[\[105\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Korea#cite_note-109)
